Overview and Roadmap

These notes provide a short unit on quantum computation in complexity theory. We will describe what quantum computation is, define $\mathsf{BQP}$, the class of problems that quantum computers can solve efficiently, and ask one main question about this class: how powerful is it? The main results we build up to answer this question by placing $\mathsf{BQP}$ between two classes: $$\mathsf{BPP}\subseteq\mathsf{BQP}\subseteq\mathsf{PP}.$$

What you will learn

By the end of these notes you should be able to:

  1. Work with qubits, unitary gates and measurements.
  2. Explain how a quantum circuit is different from a randomized one.
  3. State the definition of $\mathsf{BQP}$ and prove $\mathsf{P} \subseteq \mathsf{BPP} \subseteq \mathsf{BQP}$.
  4. Prove that amplitudes are signed counts of paths, and use that fact to prove the Main Theorem $\mathsf{BQP} \subseteq \mathsf{PP}$.

Roadmap

The chapters are meant to be read in order, since each one builds on the ones before it to get to our main theorem. We start with definitions and background knowledge, then do a warm-up proof, and finally prove the main theorem.

  1. Why Quantum Complexity?

    We go over the Extended Church–Turing thesis, why quantum computation challenges it, and why we should care about where quantum computation fits among the classes we have studied so far.

  2. Background Review

    We go over some assumed knowledge and briefly review other background we will need for our proofs. This includes complex vectors, tensor products, the class $\mathsf{PP}$, and uniform circuit families.

  3. Qubits and Quantum States

    We go over how to represent qubits as unit vectors and how to measure them. We also go over how tensor products describe multi-qubit states, and why we need $2^m$ numbers to describe $m$ qubits.

  4. Unitary Gates and Circuits

    We go over why quantum operations must be unitary, the gates $X$, $Z$, $H$, CNOT and Toffoli, and how to put gates together into quantum circuits. We also prove the Hadamard Formula, which every later chapter uses.

  5. Quantum vs. Randomized Computation

    We compare randomized and quantum computation side by side and show that the only real difference is that amplitudes can be negative. This lets computation paths cancel out. We also prove the Sum-over-Paths Lemma.

  6. $\mathsf{BQP}$ and Reversible Computation

    We define $\mathsf{BQP}$. Then we show how to make classical circuits reversible so a quantum computer can run them, which proves $\mathsf{P}\subseteq\mathsf{BQP}$ and $\mathsf{BPP}\subseteq\mathsf{BQP}$.

  7. The Main Theorem: $\mathsf{BQP}\subseteq\mathsf{PP}$

    We prove the Path-Sum Lemma, which says every amplitude is a signed count of paths. Then we prove the main theorem. The key idea is to count pairs of paths instead of single paths. The chapter also works through a small example.

  8. Conclusion and References

    We summarize what we proved, list some open problems, and collect every detail we left out along with where to find it.

Prerequisites

We assume the background of a CS455 student:

  • Turing machines, running time and space, the classes $\mathsf{P}$, $\mathsf{NP}$, $\mathsf{PSPACE}$ and $\mathsf{EXP}$, and the idea of reusing space (as in Savitch’s theorem).
  • Probabilistic TMs, the random-tape view, $\mathsf{BPP}$ and error reduction (Probabilistic Time Complexity 1 and 2).
  • Boolean circuits and circuit families, and the fact that polynomial-time TMs have polynomial-size circuits (Boolean Circuits 1 and 2).
  • Oracles (Relativization) and $\mathsf{NP}$-completeness (Reductions & Completeness).
  • Basic linear algebra and discrete probability.

Anything beyond that is reviewed in the Background Review. No physics is needed. We treat quantum mechanics as a mathematical model of computation, the same way we treated coin flips when we defined PTMs.
