Five ideas to remember

  1. Quantum = randomized with signs. Replace probabilities by amplitudes that can be negative, and stochastic matrices by unitary ones. Nothing else changes (the Quantum vs. Randomized Computation chapter).
  2. Interference is cancellation of paths, and it needs a shared endpoint. Any record of which path was taken, such as garbage or a measurement, destroys it. That is why we uncompute (the interference principle, Bennett's Uncomputation Lemma).
  3. Classical gates don't branch; Hadamards do. So a path is a choice string $b\in\{0,1\}^h$, and following it is a classical polynomial-time computation (see definition).
  4. Amplitudes are signed counts, probabilities are signed counts of pairs. That makes quantum computation a special case of counting (the Signed Counts Corollary, the Two-Path Lemma).
  5. $\mathsf{BPP}\subseteq\mathsf{BQP}\subseteq\mathsf{PP}$. Quantum computers are at least as strong as randomized ones and at most as strong as counting machines. Proving them strictly stronger would separate $\mathsf{P}$ from $\mathsf{PSPACE}$.

What is still open

  • Is $\mathsf{BPP}\ne\mathsf{BQP}$? Widely believed, because of Shor's algorithm and the oracle separations. But by the Consequences Corollary, a proof would imply $\mathsf{P}\neq\mathsf{PSPACE}$.
  • Is $\mathsf{NP}\subseteq\mathsf{BQP}$? Believed false. The BBBV lower bound shows that "black-box" search can only be sped up quadratically, so a polynomial-time quantum algorithm for SAT would have to exploit structure in a way nobody knows how to do.
  • Is $\mathsf{BQP}\subseteq\mathsf{PH}$? Unknown. False relative to an oracle (Raz–Tal). Our best general upper bounds are counting classes like $\mathsf{PP}$ (and the smaller $\mathsf{AWPP}$, the facts on further results).
  • Sampling and "quantum advantage" experiments. For sampling problems (output a sample from a distribution, rather than decide a language), there is stronger complexity-theoretic evidence. If a classical machine could sample exactly from the output distributions of certain simple quantum circuits, then the polynomial hierarchy would collapse. The argument uses $\mathsf{PostBQP}=\mathsf{PP}$ (see fact) and Toda's theorem. See Aaronson–Arkhipov (2011) and Bremner–Jozsa–Shepherd (2011). This is the theory behind recent "quantum advantage" experiments.
  • Can large quantum computers be built? The threshold theorem of quantum error correction says that a polynomial-size quantum computation can be carried out reliably on noisy hardware, provided the noise per gate is below a constant threshold. So in principle the answer is yes, and the remaining challenge is engineering (Nielsen–Chuang, Ch. 10).
