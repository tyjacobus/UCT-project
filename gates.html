Unitary Gates and Circuits

In the last chapter we defined quantum states. In this chapter we define the operations a quantum computer can apply to them, and we combine these operations into quantum circuits. We also prove the Hadamard Formula, which describes what the Hadamard gate does to a basis state. We will use it in almost every later proof.

Why operations must be unitary

In quantum mechanics, a state changes by a linear map. That map has to turn every state into a state, so it has to keep the norm of every vector equal to $1$. The matrices that do this are exactly the unitary ones.

Definition (Unitary matrix)

A square complex matrix $U$ is unitary if $U^\dagger U = I$. Equivalently, the columns of $U$ are orthonormal, and $U^{-1} = U^\dagger$. (A real unitary matrix is an orthogonal matrix: $U^TU = I$.)

Fact (Unitary = norm-preserving)

A square matrix $U$ satisfies $\|Uv\| = \|v\|$ for all vectors $v$ if and only if $U$ is unitary.

Proof. ($\Leftarrow$) $\|Uv\|^2 = (Uv)^\dagger(Uv) = v^\dagger U^\dagger U v = v^\dagger v = \|v\|^2$. ($\Rightarrow$) This is a standard linear algebra exercise: plugging in $v = e_i$, $v=e_i+e_j$ and $v = e_i+\mathrm{i}\,e_j$ shows that the columns of $U$ are orthonormal. ∎
Remark (Quantum computation is reversible)

Every unitary has an inverse ($U^\dagger$), so a quantum computer can never “forget” information. The classical AND gate sends both $00$ and $01$ to $0$, so it has no inverse and can’t be used as a quantum gate directly. This raises the question of whether quantum computers can even simulate classical ones. We answer it in the BQP and Reversible Computation chapter.

Single-qubit gates

Definition (Pauli $X$, Pauli $Z$, Hadamard $H$)

$$X = \begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix},\quad Z = \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix},\quad H = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}.$$ On basis states, for $a\in\{0,1\}$: $X\ket a = \ket{1-a}$ (the NOT gate), and $Z\ket a = (-1)^a\ket a$.

Each of these is unitary. For example, the columns of $H$, $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1,1)^T$ and $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1,-1)^T$, are orthonormal. The most important of the three is $H$, so we write down exactly what it does:

Lemma (Hadamard Formula)

For every bit $a\in\{0,1\}$, $$H\ket a = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\sum_{c\in\{0,1\}}(-1)^{a\cdot c}\ket c .$$ In words: $H$ turns a bit into an equal mix of both possible output bits, each with amplitude $\pm\tfrac1{\sqrt2}$. The sign is negative only when the input bit and the output bit are both 1.

Proof. Read off the columns of $H$: $H\ket0 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(\ket0+\ket1)$ and $H\ket1 = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket0-\ket1)$. The only minus sign is at $a=c=1$. ∎
Example ($H$ undoes itself)

By the Hadamard Formula, $H\ket0 = \ket+$ and $H\ket1 = \ket-$ (see the $\ket{\pm}$ example). Now apply $H$ to $\ket+$ and use linearity: $$H\ket+ = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}\big(H\ket0 + H\ket1\big) = \tfrac12\big(\ket0 + \ket1 + \ket0 - \ket1\big) = \ket 0.$$ The two $\ket1$ terms have opposite signs and cancel, while the two $\ket0$ terms add up. In the same way, $H\ket- = \ket1$. So $HH = I$, and $H$ can tell $\ket+$ and $\ket-$ apart perfectly, even though measuring them directly gives the same fair coin flip. This is the simplest example of interference, which is the topic of the next chapter.

Applying a gate to some qubits of a register

A circuit on $m$ qubits applies small gates (on $1$, $2$ or $3$ qubits) to a few qubits at a time. The easiest way to say what this does is on basis states: the gate acts on the bits it touches, and every other bit stays the same.

Definition (Applying a $k$-qubit gate to chosen qubits)

Let $G$ be a $2^k\times2^k$ unitary (a $k$-qubit gate) and let $j_1,\dots,j_k$ be distinct qubit positions in $\{1,\dots,m\}$. For $z\in\{0,1\}^m$ and $c\in\{0,1\}^k$, write $z[j\gets c]$ for the string $z$ with positions $j_1,\dots,j_k$ replaced by $c_1,\dots,c_k$. Then “$G$ applied to qubits $j_1,\dots,j_k$” is the $2^m\times 2^m$ matrix $G_{[j_1,\dots,j_k]}$ defined on basis states by $$G_{[j_1,\dots,j_k]}\ket z = \sum_{c\in\{0,1\}^k}\amp{c}{G}{z_{j_1}\cdots z_{j_k}}\,\ket{z[j\gets c]},$$ and extended linearly. Here $\amp cG u$ is the amplitude of $\ket c$ in $G\ket u$ (the $(c,u)$ entry of $G$).

For example, if $m=2$, then $G_{[1]} = G\otimes I$ and $G_{[2]} = I\otimes G$, by the properties of the tensor product. In general $G_{[j_1,\dots,j_k]}$ is $G\otimes I$ with the qubits reordered, so it is unitary. For instance, applying $H$ to qubit 1 of $\ket{10}$ gives $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\big(\ket{00} - \ket{10}\big)$: the first bit splits according to the Hadamard Formula and the second bit stays the same.

CNOT, Toffoli, and classical gates

Definition (CNOT and Toffoli)

For bits $a,b,c$ (where $\oplus$ is XOR): $$\CNOT\ket{a,b} = \ket{a,\;b\oplus a}, \qquad \TOF\ket{a,b,c} = \ket{a,\;b,\;c\oplus(a\wedge b)}.$$ CNOT (“controlled NOT”) flips the target bit $b$ when the control bit $a$ is $1$. Toffoli flips $c$ when both control bits are $1$. Both are their own inverses.

$X$, CNOT and Toffoli have something in common that $H$ does not: they send each basis state to a single basis state.

Definition (Classical gate)

A $k$-qubit gate $G$ is classical (or a permutation gate) if there is a permutation $\pi$ of $\{0,1\}^k$ with $G\ket u = \ket{\pi(u)}$ for every $u$. Its matrix is a permutation matrix, which is always unitary. $X$, CNOT and Toffoli are classical gates. $H$ is not.

This matters because a classical gate applied to a basis state does not split the computation. It gives exactly one basis state, which we can find by running normal reversible bit operations. So in our circuits, all splitting (and therefore all interference) comes from Hadamard gates. This is what makes the simulations in the $\mathsf{BQP}\subseteq\mathsf{PSPACE}$ and Main Theorem chapters work.

Example (Creating a Bell state)

Start with $\ket{\psi_0}=\ket{00}$. Applying $H$ to qubit 1 gives $$\ket{\psi_1} = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}\big(\ket{00} + \ket{10}\big).$$ Then apply CNOT with control qubit 1 and target qubit 2. This flips the second bit of each basis state whose first bit is $1$: $$\ket{\psi_2} = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}\big(\ket{00} + \ket{11}\big) = \ket{\Phi^+}.$$ Two gates turned a product state into the entangled Bell state. The $H$ created a superposition, and the CNOT copied the first qubit’s value into the second. (Copying basis values like this is allowed, but in the next chapter we will see that it has a cost.)

Quantum circuits and our gate set

Definition (Quantum circuit)

A quantum circuit $C$ on $m$ qubits over a finite gate set is a sequence of gates $g_1,\dots,g_T$. Each $g_t$ is a gate from the set, together with a list of distinct qubits it acts on. Let $U_t$ be the $2^m\times2^m$ unitary of $g_t$ applied to those qubits (see definition). The circuit computes the unitary $$C = U_T\,U_{T-1}\cdots U_1,$$ so $g_1$ is applied first. (We use $C$ for both the circuit and its unitary.) $T$ is the size of the circuit, and $h$ is the number of its gates that are Hadamard gates. For basis strings $x, y\in\{0,1\}^m$, $\amp yCx$ is the amplitude of $\ket y$ in $C\ket x$.

In circuit diagrams, time goes from left to right, each wire is a qubit, and qubit 1 is at the top. We only measure at the end of a circuit. This doesn’t lose anything: the principle of deferred measurement says that any measurement in the middle of a circuit can be moved to the end (by first copying the measured qubit into a fresh qubit with a CNOT) without changing the probabilities of the outcomes. See Nielsen–Chuang, §4.4.

Definition (Our gate set $\mathcal G$)

In these notes, quantum circuits use the gate set $$\mathcal G = \{\,H,\;X,\;\CNOT,\;\TOF\,\}.$$ Every gate in $\mathcal G$ except $H$ is classical (see definition), and every matrix entry is in $\{0,\pm1,\pm\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\}$. In particular, circuits over $\mathcal G$ only produce real amplitudes.

We use this gate set because it is simple and it keeps the proofs of $\mathsf{BQP}\subseteq\mathsf{PSPACE}$ and $\mathsf{BQP}\subseteq\mathsf{PP}$ clean. It also doesn’t lose any power:

Fact (Hadamard + Toffoli is universal; Shi 2003, Aharonov 2003)

Let $\mathcal G'$ be any standard universal gate set (for example, one with complex-valued gates like the phase gate $S$ or the “$\pi/8$” gate $T$). Every circuit over $\mathcal G'$ with $T$ gates can be simulated by a circuit over $\mathcal G$ with $\mathrm{poly}(T,\log(1/\varepsilon))$ gates (and some extra qubits) whose acceptance probability is within $\varepsilon$ of the original.

Remark (Omitted details)

We only use this fact to argue that our definition of $\mathsf{BQP}$ matches the standard one. Its proof combines a trick for simulating complex amplitudes with real ones (Bernstein–Vazirani 1997) with the Solovay–Kitaev theorem on efficient approximation. See Shi (2003), Aharonov (2003) and Dawson–Nielsen (2006).

This means that, for complexity purposes, we do not need complex numbers. Real amplitudes, and really just the signs $\pm$ together with the factor $1/\sqrt2$, already give all the power of quantum computation. As we will see in the next chapter, randomized computation is the same picture where every sign is $+$.

Many coins at once

Applying $H$ to each of $n$ qubits is written $H^{\otimes n}$ (it equals $H\otimes\cdots\otimes H$). We will need what it does to $\ket{0^n}$:

Lemma (Uniform Superposition Lemma)

$$H^{\otimes n}\ket{0^n} = \frac{1}{2^{n/2}}\sum_{y\in\{0,1\}^n}\ket y .$$

Proof. By the side-by-side fact and property (3) of the tensor product, $H^{\otimes n}\ket{0^n} = H\ket0\otimes\cdots\otimes H\ket0 = \ket+^{\otimes n}$. Expanding $\big(\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket0+\ket1)\big)^{\otimes n}$ by bilinearity gives one term $2^{-n/2}\ket{y_1\cdots y_n}$ for each $y\in\{0,1\}^n$. ∎

So $n$ Hadamard gates turn $\ket{0^n}$ into $n$ “quantum coins”: measuring them would give a uniformly random $n$-bit string.

Summary

  • Quantum operations are unitary. That means they keep norms the same, and they are always reversible.
  • $H\ket a = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}\sum_c(-1)^{ac}\ket c$. Hadamard gates split the computation in two, with a minus sign only on the $1\to1$ branch.
  • Classical gates ($X$, CNOT, Toffoli) just rearrange basis states, so they never split the computation.
