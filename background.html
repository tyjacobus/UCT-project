Guiding Question

What is the class $\mathsf{PP}$, and why is it a natural home for "counting" problems?

Complex vectors and inner products

A complex number is $\alpha = a + bi$ with $a,b\in\R$ and $i^2=-1$. Its conjugate is $\bar\alpha = a - bi$, and its modulus is $|\alpha| = \sqrt{a^2+b^2}$. The one identity we really use is $$|\alpha|^2 = \alpha\,\bar\alpha .$$ For a real number, $|\alpha|^2 = \alpha^2$. That will be the only case we need for the main theorem: our gate set (see definition) only produces real amplitudes.

Definition (Conjugate transpose, inner product, norm)

For a complex matrix $A$, its conjugate transpose $A^\dagger$ is obtained by transposing $A$ and conjugating every entry: $(A^\dagger)_{ij} = \overline{A_{ji}}$. For $u, v\in\C^N$ (column vectors), the inner product and norm are $$\langle u, v\rangle = u^\dagger v = \sum_{i=1}^N \overline{u_i}\, v_i, \qquad \|v\| = \sqrt{\langle v,v\rangle} = \Big(\sum_{i=1}^N |v_i|^2\Big)^{1/2}.$$ A unit vector has $\|v\|=1$. The standard basis vectors $e_1,\dots,e_N$ are orthonormal: $\langle e_i, e_j\rangle = 1$ if $i=j$ and $0$ otherwise.

For real vectors, all of this is the ordinary dot product and Euclidean length from your linear algebra course.

Tensor products

Quantum computing uses the tensor product to "put systems side by side." If one system is described by a vector in $\C^N$ and another by a vector in $\C^M$, the pair is described by a vector in $\C^{NM}$, with one coordinate for every pair of indices.

Definition (Tensor (Kronecker) product)

For $u\in\C^N$ and $v\in\C^M$, the tensor product $u\otimes v\in\C^{NM}$ has coordinates indexed by pairs $(i,j)$, listed in lexicographic order: $$(u\otimes v)_{(i,j)} = u_i\, v_j.$$ Similarly, for an $N\times N$ matrix $A$ and an $M\times M$ matrix $B$, $A\otimes B$ is the $NM\times NM$ matrix with $(A\otimes B)_{(i,j),(i',j')} = A_{ii'}\,B_{jj'}$. In block form, $A\otimes B = \begin{pmatrix} A_{11}B & \cdots & A_{1N}B\\ \vdots & & \vdots \\ A_{N1}B & \cdots & A_{NN}B\end{pmatrix}$.

Example

$\begin{pmatrix}a\\b\end{pmatrix}\otimes\begin{pmatrix}c\\d\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}ac\\ad\\bc\\bd\end{pmatrix}$, with coordinates indexed by the pairs $(1,1),(1,2),(2,1),(2,2)$. For instance $\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix}\otimes\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}0\\1\\0\\0\end{pmatrix}$: the tensor product of two standard basis vectors is a standard basis vector.

Fact (Properties of $\otimes$)
  1. Bilinear: $(\alpha u + \beta u')\otimes v = \alpha(u\otimes v) + \beta(u'\otimes v)$, and the same in the second slot.
  2. Basis to basis: $e_i\otimes e_j = e_{(i,j)}$.
  3. Acts factor by factor: $(A\otimes B)(u\otimes v) = (Au)\otimes(Bv)$, and $(A\otimes B)(C\otimes D) = (AC)\otimes(BD)$.
  4. Norms multiply: $\|u\otimes v\| = \|u\|\,\|v\|$.
  5. Associative: $(u\otimes v)\otimes w = u\otimes(v\otimes w)$, so we can drop parentheses.
Proof sketch. Each item follows by comparing coordinates. For example, for item 3: $\big((A\otimes B)(u\otimes v)\big)_{(i,j)} = \sum_{i',j'} A_{ii'}B_{jj'}u_{i'}v_{j'} = \big(\sum_{i'}A_{ii'}u_{i'}\big)\big(\sum_{j'}B_{jj'}v_{j'}\big) = (Au)_i (Bv)_j$. For item 4, $\sum_{i,j}|u_iv_j|^2 = \sum_i |u_i|^2\sum_j|v_j|^2$. ∎

Randomized classes: BPP and PP

Recall from Probabilistic Time Complexity 1 that a probabilistic TM (PTM) is a TM with additional coin-flip states, and that it can equivalently be viewed through the random-tape view: a deterministic TM $M(x,r)$ that takes the random bits $r$ as a second input. We will only care about polynomial-time, always-halting PTMs, and without loss of generality we assume that on inputs of length $n$ the machine reads exactly $p(n)$ random bits for some polynomial $p$ (pad with unused bits if needed). Then $$\Pr[M(x)\text{ accepts}] = \Pr_{r\sim\{0,1\}^{p(|x|)}}[M(x,r)\text{ accepts}],$$ which is simply the fraction of random strings $r$ that lead to acceptance.

Definition ($\mathsf{BPP}$, recalled from CS455)

$$\mathsf{BPTIME}(t(n)) = \{L \mid \exists\text{ PTM } M \text{ solving } L \text{ in time } O(t(n)) \text{ and error } \tfrac13\},$$ $$\mathsf{BPP} = \bigcup_{k\in\mathbb N}\mathsf{BPTIME}(n^k).$$ Recall that $M$ solves $L$ with error $\epsilon$ if $$\forall x\in L,\ \Pr[M(x)\text{ accepts}]\ge 1-\epsilon, \qquad \forall x\notin L,\ \Pr[M(x)\text{ accepts}]\le\epsilon.$$ By the Two-sided Error Reduction theorem from Probabilistic Time Complexity 2 (proved with Hoeffding’s inequality), the constant $\tfrac13$ can be replaced by any constant in $(0,\tfrac12)$, or even by $2^{-\mathrm{poly}(n)}$.

The next class is not covered in CS455. It drops the requirement that there be a gap between yes-instances and no-instances.

Definition ($\mathsf{PP}$, probabilistic polynomial time; Gill 1977)

$L\in\mathsf{PP}$ if there is a polynomial-time PTM $M$ such that $$\forall x\in L,\quad \Pr[M(x)\text{ accepts}] > \tfrac12, \qquad\qquad \forall x\notin L,\quad \Pr[M(x)\text{ accepts}]\le\tfrac12 .$$

Recall that achieving error exactly $\tfrac12$ is trivial: the algorithm that returns $\mathrm{Bern}(1/2)$ does it. A $\mathsf{PP}$ machine has to do just a tiny bit better than that, and only on yes-instances.

Remark ($\mathsf{PP}$ is majority vote with no margin)

A $\mathsf{BPP}$ machine must be right with a noticeable margin, so you can run it a few times and trust the majority. A $\mathsf{PP}$ machine only has to tip the balance by the smallest possible amount, perhaps a single random string out of $2^{p(n)}$. You can't detect such a bias by sampling. What a $\mathsf{PP}$ machine really does is decide whether an exponentially large count (of accepting random strings) is above a threshold. The canonical $\mathsf{PP}$-complete problem is $$\mathrm{MAJSAT} = \{\langle\varphi\rangle : \text{more than half of all assignments satisfy the Boolean formula } \varphi\}.$$ $\mathsf{PP}$ is a very powerful class: by Toda’s theorem, the whole polynomial hierarchy is contained in $\mathsf{P}^{\mathsf{PP}}$.

Proposition ($\mathsf{BPP}\subseteq\mathsf{PP}\subseteq\mathsf{PSPACE}$)

$$\mathsf{BPP}\subseteq\mathsf{PP}\subseteq\mathsf{PSPACE}.$$

Proof.

If $M$ solves $L$ with error $\tfrac13$, the same $M$ witnesses $L\in\mathsf{PP}$, since $\ge \tfrac23$ implies $>\tfrac12$ and $\le\tfrac13$ implies $\le\tfrac12$.

For $\mathsf{PP}\subseteq\mathsf{PSPACE}$, let $L\in\mathsf{PP}$ via $M(x,r)$ with $r\in\{0,1\}^{p(|x|)}$. We define $M'$ as follows (compare with the proof of the trivial derandomization $\mathsf{BPP}\subseteq\mathsf{EXP}$):

def $M'(x)$:
$\mathit{count} \gets 0$
for all $r\in\{0,1\}^{p(|x|)}$:
run $M(x,r)$; if it accepts: $\mathit{count}\gets\mathit{count}+1$
if $\mathit{count} > 2^{p(|x|)-1}$: accept
else: reject

Each run of $M(x,r)$ takes polynomial time, hence polynomial space, and the same work space is reused for every $r$. Besides that, $M'$ stores the current $r$ ($p(|x|)$ bits) and the counter ($p(|x|)+1$ bits). So $M'$ runs in polynomial space, and it accepts exactly when $\Pr[M(x)\text{ accepts}]>\tfrac12$. ∎

Exercise (A first taste of the abstain trick)

Show that $\mathsf{NP}\subseteq\mathsf{PP}$. Hint: on input a formula $\varphi$ with $k$ variables, pick a random assignment $a$ and one extra random bit $c$.

Let $M$ pick $a\in\{0,1\}^k$ and $c\in\{0,1\}$ uniformly at random. If $\varphi(a)=1$, accept. Otherwise accept if and only if $c=0$, so on these branches $M$ “abstains” by accepting exactly half of the time. If $s$ is the number of satisfying assignments, then $$\Pr[M(\varphi)\text{ accepts}] = \frac{s}{2^k} + \Big(1-\frac{s}{2^k}\Big)\cdot\frac12 = \frac12 + \frac{s}{2^{k+1}},$$ which is $>\tfrac12$ if and only if $s\ge1$, that is, if and only if $\varphi\in\mathrm{SAT}$. Since $\mathrm{SAT}$ is $\mathsf{NP}$-complete under polynomial-time reductions (Reductions & Completeness 1), and $\mathsf{PP}$ is closed under them, $\mathsf{NP}\subseteq\mathsf{PP}$. The same trick (accept on some branches, reject on others, and flip a fair coin on the rest so they contribute exactly $\tfrac12$) is the engine of the proof of the Main Theorem: see the Signed Counting Lemma.

Uniform circuit families

Recall from Boolean Circuits 1 that a Boolean circuit $C_n$ with $n$ inputs is a straight-line program of AND, OR and NOT instructions, and that a circuit family $\mathcal C = (C_0, C_1, C_2,\dots)$ has one circuit for each input length. In Boolean Circuits 2 we saw the power of such a non-uniform model: any sparse language, even an undecidable one, can be hard-coded into a circuit family, which is why $\mathsf{P}\subsetneq\mathsf{P}/\mathsf{poly}$. To use circuits as a model of algorithms, we must forbid this, and require that the circuits be easy to construct.

Definition (Polynomial-time uniform circuit family)

A circuit family $\mathcal C = (C_0,C_1,C_2,\dots)$ is polynomial-time uniform if there is a deterministic TM that, on input $1^n$, outputs a description of $C_n$ (the list of its gates and the wires each gate acts on) in time polynomial in $n$. In particular, $C_n$ has $\mathrm{poly}(n)$ gates.

The same definition will apply to quantum circuits (see the definition of $\mathsf{BQP}$). The only thing that changes is which gates are allowed. The circuit families we get from Turing machines are uniform:

Theorem (Efficient TM to efficient uniform circuit)

Let $V$ be a TM that runs in polynomial time and outputs one bit. Then there is a polynomial-time uniform circuit family $\mathcal D = (D_0, D_1, \dots)$ with gates AND, OR, NOT of fan-in at most $2$, such that $D_N(w) = V(w)$ for every $N$ and every $w\in\{0,1\}^N$.

Remark (Omitted details)

This is the Efficient TM to Efficient Circuit theorem from Boolean Circuits 1 ($\mathsf{P}\subseteq\mathsf{P}/\mathsf{poly}$), together with the observation that its circuits can be printed efficiently. Each cell of the $t(N)\times t(N)$ computation tableau is computed from the cells above it by a constant-size circuit, and the wiring is so regular that a TM can output $D_N$ in time $\mathrm{poly}(N)$. See Sipser, Introduction to the Theory of Computation (3rd ed.), Section 9.3, or Arora–Barak, Computational Complexity: A Modern Approach, Chapter 6.

Summary

  • $\langle u,v\rangle = \sum_i \overline{u_i}v_i$ and $\|v\|^2 = \sum_i |v_i|^2$. Quantum states will be unit vectors in this norm.
  • $u\otimes v$ has one coordinate $u_iv_j$ for every pair $(i,j)$. Tensoring basis vectors gives basis vectors, and $(A\otimes B)(u\otimes v) = Au\otimes Bv$.
  • $\mathsf{PP}$ = "accept with probability $>1/2$ exactly on yes-instances," with no gap required. It is really a class about comparing exponentially large counts, and it sits inside $\mathsf{PSPACE}$.
  • Polynomial-time TMs can be converted efficiently into polynomial-size circuits (the Efficient TM to Efficient Circuit theorem). We'll make these circuits reversible in the BQP and Reversible Computation chapter.
