Overview and Roadmap

Welcome! These notes are a short, self-contained unit on quantum computation from the point of view of complexity theory, written for students of CS455. The main question is the one we ask about every new model of computation: how powerful is it? We will define the class $\mathsf{BQP}$ carefully, and then trap it between two classical classes: $$\mathsf{BPP}\subseteq\mathsf{BQP}\subseteq\mathsf{PP}.$$

Guiding Question

Are quantum computers more powerful than classical randomized computers? If so, by how much?

The story in one paragraph

Recall that a randomized algorithm induces a probability tree: every root-to-leaf path has a probability, and the probability of an outcome is the sum over the paths that lead to it. A quantum algorithm does the same thing with amplitudes, which are allowed to be negative. Positive and negative contributions can cancel, and that cancellation (called interference) is the only new ingredient. It is enough to give quantum computers a fast algorithm for factoring, which nobody knows how to do classically. On the other hand, a sum over paths with signs is exactly the kind of thing a classical counting machine can evaluate. Making that precise proves the main theorem of these notes.

Theorem (Main Theorem; Adleman, DeMarrais and Huang, 1997)

$$\mathsf{BQP} \subseteq \mathsf{PP}.$$ Every language solved by a polynomial-time quantum algorithm with bounded error is solved by a polynomial-time PTM that accepts with probability more than $\tfrac12$ exactly on members of the language.

It is stated precisely and proved in full in the Main Theorem chapter. Here is the whole picture we will build. Every inclusion is proved in these notes except the first one, which you already know from CS455.

What you will learn

By the end of these notes you should be able to:

  1. Compute with qubits, unitary gates and measurements, and explain exactly how a quantum circuit differs from a randomized one. The difference is signs, and the cancellation they allow.
  2. State the definition of $\mathsf{BQP}$ precisely and prove $\mathsf{P} \subseteq \mathsf{BPP} \subseteq \mathsf{BQP}$, using reversible computation and Bennett’s uncomputation trick.
  3. Prove that amplitudes are signed counts of paths (the Path-Sum Lemma), and use it to show $\mathsf{BQP} \subseteq \mathsf{PSPACE}$.
  4. Prove the Main Theorem $\mathsf{BQP} \subseteq \mathsf{PP}$, and explain why the proof needs pairs of paths.

Roadmap

The chapters are meant to be read in order, using the sidebar on the left. Together they are about two CS455 lectures’ worth of material. The first lecture (Qubits through $\mathsf{BQP}$ and Reversible Computation) builds the model and ends with the definition of $\mathsf{BQP}$ and the lower bound $\mathsf{BPP}\subseteq\mathsf{BQP}$. The second lecture ($\mathsf{BQP}\subseteq\mathsf{PSPACE}$ and the Main Theorem) proves the upper bounds.

  1. Why Quantum Complexity?

    The Extended Church–Turing thesis, where quantum computing sits relative to the classes we know from CS455, and why complexity theorists care.

  2. Background Review

    What we assume from CS455, plus short reviews of what goes beyond it: complex vectors, tensor products, the class $\mathsf{PP}$, and uniform circuit families. Skim it now, and come back when a later chapter links here.

  3. Qubits and Quantum States

    Qubits as unit vectors, Dirac notation, measurement, multi-qubit states via tensor products, entanglement (the Bell state), and why $m$ qubits need $2^m$ numbers to describe.

  4. Unitary Gates and Circuits

    Why operations must be unitary; the gates $X$, $Z$, $H$, CNOT and Toffoli with worked examples; the definition of a quantum circuit and of our gate set $\mathcal G$; and the Hadamard Formula that every later chapter uses.

  5. Quantum vs. Randomized Computation

    Randomized computation as stochastic matrices, quantum computation as unitary matrices. Flipping a coin twice versus applying $H$ twice. The Sum-over-Paths Lemma, and two ways to destroy interference.

  6. $\mathsf{BQP}$ and Reversible Computation

    The definition of $\mathsf{BQP}$. Reversible computing with Toffoli gates, garbage, and Bennett’s uncomputation trick, giving $\mathsf{P}\subseteq\mathsf{BQP}$ and $\mathsf{BPP}\subseteq\mathsf{BQP}$.

  7. $\mathsf{BQP}\subseteq\mathsf{PSPACE}$

    Schrödinger-style simulation ($\mathsf{BQP}\subseteq\mathsf{EXP}$) versus Feynman-style simulation. The Path-Sum Lemma: every amplitude is $2^{-h/2}$ times a signed count of paths. Then $\mathsf{BQP}\subseteq\mathsf{PSPACE}$ by reusing space.

  8. The Main Theorem: $\mathsf{BQP}\subseteq\mathsf{PP}$

    Why squaring amplitudes is the obstacle, and how pairs of paths get around it; the abstain trick for signed counting in $\mathsf{PP}$; the full proof, with an exact formula for the $\mathsf{PP}$ machine’s acceptance probability; and an interactive explorer.

  9. Conclusion and References

    What is open (is $\mathsf{BPP} \ne \mathsf{BQP}$? is $\mathsf{NP}\subseteq\mathsf{BQP}$?), why proving quantum advantage unconditionally would separate $\mathsf{P}$ from $\mathsf{PSPACE}$, every omitted detail with its source, and all references.

Prerequisites

We assume exactly the background of a CS455 student:

  • Turing machines, running time and space; the classes $\mathsf{P}$, $\mathsf{NP}$, $\mathsf{PSPACE}$, $\mathsf{EXP}$; the idea of reusing space (as in Savitch’s theorem).
  • Probabilistic TMs, the random-tape view, $\mathsf{BPP}$, and error reduction (Probabilistic Time Complexity 1 and 2).
  • Boolean circuits and circuit families, and the fact that polynomial-time TMs have polynomial-size circuits (Boolean Circuits 1 and 2).
  • Oracles and relativization (Relativization), and $\mathsf{NP}$-completeness (Reductions & Completeness).
  • Basic linear algebra (vectors, matrices, matrix multiplication) and discrete probability.

Everything beyond that is reviewed in the Background Review chapter: complex vectors, tensor products, the class $\mathsf{PP}$ (which CS455 does not cover), and uniform circuit families. No physics is assumed. We treat quantum mechanics as a mathematical model of computation, in the same way that we treated coin flips when we defined PTMs.
