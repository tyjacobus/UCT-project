Five ideas to remember

  1. Quantum = randomized with signs. Swap probabilities for amplitudes that can be negative, and stochastic matrices for unitary ones. Everything else stays the same (the Quantum vs. Randomized Computation chapter).
  2. Interference is cancellation of paths, and it needs a shared endpoint. If anything records which path was taken, like leftover garbage or a measurement, the interference goes away. That is why we uncompute (the interference principle, Bennett's Uncomputation Lemma).
  3. Classical gates don't branch; Hadamards do. So a path is a choice string $b\in\{0,1\}^h$, and following it is a classical polynomial-time computation (see definition).
  4. Amplitudes are signed counts, probabilities are signed counts of pairs. That makes quantum computation a special case of counting (the Signed Counts Corollary, the Two-Path Lemma).
  5. $\mathsf{BPP}\subseteq\mathsf{BQP}\subseteq\mathsf{PP}$. Quantum computers are at least as strong as randomized ones and at most as strong as counting machines. Proving they are strictly stronger than randomized ones would also prove $\mathsf{P}\ne\mathsf{PSPACE}$.

What is still open

  • Is $\mathsf{BPP}\ne\mathsf{BQP}$? Most people believe so, because of Shor’s algorithm and the oracle separations. But by the Consequences Corollary, a proof would imply $\mathsf{P}\neq\mathsf{PSPACE}$.
  • Is $\mathsf{NP}\subseteq\mathsf{BQP}$? Most people believe not. Quantum computers can only speed up black-box search quadratically (Grover; Bennett et al.), so a fast quantum algorithm for SAT would have to use the structure of SAT in a way nobody knows how to.
  • Is $\mathsf{BQP}\subseteq\mathsf{PH}$? Nobody knows, and it is false relative to an oracle (Raz–Tal). The best upper bounds we have are counting classes like $\mathsf{PP}$ (see the further results).
