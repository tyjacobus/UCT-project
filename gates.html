Unitary Gates and Circuits

In the last chapter we defined quantum states. In this chapter we define the operations a quantum computer may apply to them, and we put them together into quantum circuits. The most important formula of these notes appears here: the action of the Hadamard gate on a basis state. It is the heart of the path-sum argument we will use to prove the main theorem.

Guiding Question

Which operations are allowed on a quantum state, and why?

Guiding Question

Which gates branch the computation, and which do not?

Why operations must be unitary

The rules of quantum mechanics say that (between measurements) a state evolves by a linear map. That map must send every state to a state, so it must preserve the norm of every vector. Linear algebra tells us exactly which matrices do that.

Definition (Unitary matrix)

A square complex matrix $U$ is unitary if $U^\dagger U = I$. Equivalently, the columns of $U$ are orthonormal, and $U^{-1} = U^\dagger$. (A real unitary matrix is an orthogonal matrix: $U^TU = I$.)

Fact (Unitary = norm-preserving)

A square matrix $U$ satisfies $\|Uv\| = \|v\|$ for all vectors $v$ if and only if $U$ is unitary.

Proof. ($\Leftarrow$) $\|Uv\|^2 = (Uv)^\dagger(Uv) = v^\dagger U^\dagger U v = v^\dagger v = \|v\|^2$. ($\Rightarrow$) Write $u_i = Ue_i$ for the columns. Taking $v=e_i$ gives $\|u_i\| = 1$. For $i\ne j$, expanding gives $\|u_i+u_j\|^2 = 2 + 2\,\mathrm{Re}\langle u_i,u_j\rangle$, and this must equal $\|e_i+e_j\|^2 = 2$, so $\mathrm{Re}\langle u_i,u_j\rangle=0$. Similarly $\|u_i + \mathrm{i}\,u_j\|^2 = 2 - 2\,\mathrm{Im}\langle u_i,u_j\rangle$ must equal $2$, so $\mathrm{Im}\langle u_i,u_j\rangle = 0$. So the columns are orthonormal. ∎
Remark (Quantum computation is reversible)

Every unitary has an inverse ($U^\dagger$), so a quantum computer can never "forget" information. The classical AND gate maps both $01$ and $00$ to $0$, so it has no inverse and can't be a quantum gate as it stands. This looks like a serious handicap. Can quantum computers even simulate classical ones? The BQP and Reversible Computation chapter shows how to get around it.

Single-qubit gates

Definition (Pauli $X$, Pauli $Z$, phase $S$, Hadamard $H$)

$$X = \begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix},\quad Z = \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix},\quad S = \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&\mathrm{i}\end{pmatrix},\quad H = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}.$$ On basis states, for $a\in\{0,1\}$: $X\ket a = \ket{1-a}$ (the NOT gate), $Z\ket a = (-1)^a\ket a$, and $S\ket a = \mathrm{i}^a\ket a$.

Each of these is unitary. For $H$, for instance, the columns $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1,1)^T$ and $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1,-1)^T$ are orthonormal. The most important one by far is $H$, so let's record its action in a form we'll quote again and again.

Lemma (Hadamard Formula)

For every bit $a\in\{0,1\}$, $$H\ket a = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\sum_{c\in\{0,1\}}(-1)^{a\cdot c}\ket c .$$ In words: $H$ sends a bit to an equal superposition of both possible output bits, with amplitude $\pm\tfrac1{\sqrt2}$. The sign is negative exactly when the input bit and the output bit are both 1.

Proof. Read off the columns of $H$: $H\ket0 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(\ket0+\ket1)$ and $H\ket1 = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket0-\ket1)$. The only minus sign is at $a=c=1$. ∎
Example ($H$ undoes itself, and the first cancellation)

From the Hadamard Formula, $H\ket0 = \ket+$ and $H\ket1 = \ket-$ (see the $\ket{\pm}$ example). Now apply $H$ to $\ket+$ and use linearity: $$H\ket+ = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}\big(H\ket0 + H\ket1\big) = \tfrac12\big(\ket0 + \ket1 + \ket0 - \ket1\big) = \ket 0.$$ The two contributions to $\ket1$ have opposite signs and cancel, while the two contributions to $\ket0$ add up. Similarly $H\ket- = \ket1$. So $HH = I$, and $H$ tells $\ket+$ and $\ket-$ apart perfectly, even though measuring them directly gives identical fair coin flips. Keep this example in mind. It is the simplest instance of interference, which the Quantum vs. Randomized Computation chapter is about.

Applying a gate to some qubits of a register

A circuit on $m$ qubits applies small gates (acting on $1$, $2$ or $3$ qubits) to a few qubits at a time. The cleanest way to say what this does is on basis states: the gate acts on the bits it touches, and every other bit comes along unchanged.

Definition (Applying a $k$-qubit gate to chosen qubits)

Let $G$ be a $2^k\times2^k$ unitary (a $k$-qubit gate) and let $j_1,\dots,j_k$ be distinct qubit positions in $\{1,\dots,m\}$. For $z\in\{0,1\}^m$ and $c\in\{0,1\}^k$, write $z[j\gets c]$ for the string $z$ with positions $j_1,\dots,j_k$ overwritten by $c_1,\dots,c_k$. Then "$G$ applied to qubits $j_1,\dots,j_k$" is the $2^m\times 2^m$ matrix $G_{[j_1,\dots,j_k]}$ defined on basis states by $$G_{[j_1,\dots,j_k]}\ket z = \sum_{c\in\{0,1\}^k}\amp{c}{G}{z_{j_1}\cdots z_{j_k}}\,\ket{z[j\gets c]},$$ and extended linearly. Here $\amp cG u$ is the amplitude of $\ket c$ in $G\ket u$ (that is, the $(c,u)$ entry of $G$).

For example, if $m=2$, then $G_{[1]} = G\otimes I$ and $G_{[2]} = I\otimes G$, by the properties of the tensor product. In general $G_{[j_1,\dots,j_k]}$ is $G\otimes I$ with the qubits reordered, so it is unitary. As a small example, $H$ applied to qubit 1 of $\ket{10}$ gives $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\big(\ket{00} - \ket{10}\big)$: the first bit branches according to the Hadamard Formula and the second bit is carried along.

CNOT, Toffoli, and classical gates

Definition (CNOT and Toffoli)

For bits $a,b,c$ (where $\oplus$ is XOR): $$\CNOT\ket{a,b} = \ket{a,\;b\oplus a}, \qquad \TOF\ket{a,b,c} = \ket{a,\;b,\;c\oplus(a\wedge b)}.$$ CNOT ("controlled NOT") flips the target bit $b$ exactly when the control bit $a$ is $1$. Toffoli ("controlled-controlled NOT") flips $c$ exactly when both controls are $1$. Both are their own inverses.

$X$, CNOT and TOF have something in common that $H$ lacks: they send each basis state to a single basis state.

Definition (Classical gate)

A $k$-qubit gate $G$ is classical (or a permutation gate) if there is a permutation $\pi$ of $\{0,1\}^k$ with $G\ket u = \ket{\pi(u)}$ for every $u$. Its matrix is a permutation matrix, which is automatically unitary. $X$, CNOT and TOF are classical gates; $H$ is not.

The reason to single out classical gates: when you apply one to a basis state, the computation does not branch. Applied to qubits of $\ket z$, a classical gate produces exactly one basis state, which you can compute by running ordinary reversible bit operations on $z$. In our circuits, all branching (and so all interference) comes from Hadamard gates. This observation is what makes the simulations in the last two technical chapters work.

Example (Creating a Bell state)

Start in $\ket{\psi_0}=\ket{00}$. Apply $H$ to qubit 1 (the Hadamard Formula and the definition of applying a gate to chosen qubits): $$\ket{\psi_1} = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}\big(\ket{00} + \ket{10}\big).$$ Apply CNOT with control qubit 1 and target qubit 2, which flips the second bit of each basis state whose first bit is $1$: $$\ket{\psi_2} = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}\big(\ket{00} + \ket{11}\big) = \ket{\Phi^+}.$$ Two gates turned a product state into the entangled Bell state of the proposition that the Bell state is entangled. The $H$ created a superposition, and the CNOT copied the first qubit's basis value into the second. (Copying basis values is fine. In the Quantum vs. Randomized Computation chapter we'll see that it has a price.)

Quantum circuits and our gate set

Definition (Quantum circuit)

A quantum circuit $C$ on $m$ qubits over a finite gate set is a sequence of gates $g_1,\dots,g_T$. Each $g_t$ is a gate from the set together with a list of distinct qubits it acts on. Let $U_t$ be the $2^m\times2^m$ unitary of $g_t$ applied to those qubits (the definition of applying a gate to chosen qubits). The circuit computes the unitary $$C = U_T\,U_{T-1}\cdots U_1,$$ so $g_1$ is applied first. (We use the letter $C$ for both the circuit and its unitary.) $T$ is the size of the circuit, and $h$ denotes the number of its gates that are Hadamard gates. For basis strings $x, y\in\{0,1\}^m$, $\amp yCx$ is the amplitude of $\ket y$ in $C\ket x$.

In diagrams, time flows left to right, each wire is a qubit, and qubit 1 is on top. We only ever measure at the end of a circuit. This loses no generality: the principle of deferred measurement says that any intermediate measurement can be postponed to the end (by copying the measured qubit's value into a fresh qubit with a CNOT) without changing the distribution of outcomes. See Nielsen–Chuang, §4.4.

Definition (Our gate set $\mathcal G$)

Throughout these notes, quantum circuits use the gate set $$\mathcal G = \{\,H,\;X,\;\CNOT,\;\TOF\,\}.$$ Every gate in $\mathcal G$ except $H$ is classical (the definition of classical gates), and every matrix entry is in $\{0,\pm1,\pm\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\}$. In particular, all amplitudes produced by circuits over $\mathcal G$ are real numbers.

Why this choice? It is about as simple as possible and makes the proofs of $\mathsf{BQP}\subseteq\mathsf{PSPACE}$ and $\mathsf{BQP}\subseteq\mathsf{PP}$ clean. It is also no restriction at all, as far as complexity is concerned:

Fact (Hadamard + Toffoli is universal; Shi 2003, Aharonov 2003)

Let $\mathcal G'$ be any standard universal gate set (for example, one containing $S$, the "$\pi/8$" gate $T$, or rotations with efficiently computable complex entries). Every circuit over $\mathcal G'$ with $T$ gates can be simulated by a circuit over $\mathcal G$ with $\mathrm{poly}(T,\log(1/\varepsilon))$ gates (and some extra qubits) whose acceptance probability differs by at most $\varepsilon$.

Remark (Omitted details)

We use the universality theorem for Hadamard + Toffoli only to argue that our definition of $\mathsf{BQP}$ (see definition) is the standard one. Its proof has two ingredients: (i) complex amplitudes can be simulated by real ones using one extra qubit that holds "real part vs. imaginary part" (Bernstein–Vazirani 1997); and (ii) $\{H,\TOF\}$ generates a dense set of real orthogonal transformations (on an encoded subspace), and the Solovay–Kitaev theorem turns density into efficient approximation. See Y. Shi, "Both Toffoli and controlled-NOT need little help to do universal quantum computation" (2003); D. Aharonov, "A simple proof that Toffoli and Hadamard are quantum universal" (2003); and Dawson–Nielsen, "The Solovay–Kitaev algorithm" (2006).

Remark (Negative numbers are the whole story)

The universality theorem for Hadamard + Toffoli has a striking interpretation: for complexity purposes, complex numbers are not needed. Real amplitudes, and in fact just the signs $\pm$ together with the scaling $1/\sqrt2$, already give the full power of quantum computation. Compare this with randomized computation, which is the same picture with all signs $+$ (the Quantum vs. Randomized Computation chapter).

The Hadamard transform

Applying $H$ to each of $n$ qubits is so common that it gets its own notation, $H^{\otimes n}$ (it equals $H\otimes\cdots\otimes H$). Its action on basis states follows from the Hadamard Formula:

Lemma (Hadamard Transform Lemma)

For every $x\in\{0,1\}^n$, $$H^{\otimes n}\ket x = \frac{1}{2^{n/2}}\sum_{y\in\{0,1\}^n}(-1)^{x\cdot y}\ket y, \qquad\text{where } x\cdot y = x_1y_1+\cdots+x_ny_n \bmod 2.$$ In particular, $H^{\otimes n}\ket{0^n} = 2^{-n/2}\sum_y\ket y$ is the uniform superposition over all $n$-bit strings ("$n$ quantum coins").

Proof. By the "side by side = tensor product" fact, $\ket x = \ket{x_1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\ket{x_n}$, and by the property (3) of the tensor product, $H^{\otimes n}$ acts on each factor separately. Using the Hadamard Formula on each factor, $$H^{\otimes n}\ket x = \bigotimes_{i=1}^n\Big(\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\sum_{y_i\in\{0,1\}}(-1)^{x_iy_i}\ket{y_i}\Big) = \frac{1}{2^{n/2}}\sum_{y_1,\dots,y_n}(-1)^{x_1y_1+\cdots+x_ny_n}\ket{y_1\cdots y_n},$$ where the last step expands the tensor product by bilinearity (the property (1) of the tensor product) and uses the "side by side = tensor product" fact again. ∎

Exercises

Exercise

Verify that $HXH = Z$ and $HZH = X$. (So $Z$ is available in our gate set even though it isn't listed.)

On basis states: $HXH\ket a$. Let's do $a=0$: $H\ket0 = \ket+$, $X\ket+ = \ket+$, and $H\ket+ = \ket0 = Z\ket0$. For $a=1$: $H\ket1 = \ket-$, $X\ket- = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket1-\ket0) = -\ket-$, and $H(-\ket-) = -\ket1 = Z\ket1$. Then $HZH = H(HXH)H = X$, since $HH=I$.

Exercise

Let $C$ be the 2-qubit circuit "$H$ on both qubits, then CNOT with control 1 and target 2, then $H$ on both qubits." Show that $C$ is CNOT with control 2 and target 1.

By the Hadamard Transform Lemma, $H^{\otimes2}\ket{ab} = \tfrac12\sum_{y_1,y_2}(-1)^{ay_1+by_2}\ket{y_1y_2}$. CNOT maps $\ket{y_1y_2}\mapsto\ket{y_1,y_1\oplus y_2}$. Substituting $y_2' = y_1\oplus y_2$ (so $y_2 = y_1\oplus y_2'$), and using $(-1)^{b(y_1\oplus y_2')} = (-1)^{by_1 + by_2'}$, the state becomes $\tfrac12\sum_{y_1,y_2'}(-1)^{(a\oplus b)y_1 + by_2'}\ket{y_1y_2'} = H^{\otimes2}\ket{a\oplus b,\;b}$. Applying $H^{\otimes 2}$ again (it is its own inverse) gives $\ket{a\oplus b,\,b}$, which is CNOT with control 2 and target 1. Interesting: in the "Hadamard basis," the roles of control and target swap.

Summary

  • Quantum operations are unitary, which means norm-preserving and therefore reversible.
  • $H\ket a = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}\sum_c(-1)^{ac}\ket c$. Hadamard gates branch, with a minus sign exactly on the $1\to1$ branch.
  • Classical gates ($X$, CNOT, Toffoli) permute basis states, so they never branch.
  • A circuit is a product of gate unitaries, $C = U_T\cdots U_1$. We use $\mathcal G = \{H,X,\CNOT,\TOF\}$, which has real amplitudes and loses nothing (the universality theorem for Hadamard + Toffoli).
  • $H^{\otimes n}\ket x = 2^{-n/2}\sum_y(-1)^{x\cdot y}\ket y$.
