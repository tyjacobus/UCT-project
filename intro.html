Why Quantum Complexity?

Before we write down a single qubit, let’s explain why a complexity theorist should care about quantum computation at all, and how it fits into the landscape of classes we have been building in CS455. There are no theorems in this chapter, just the questions that the remaining chapters answer.

Guiding Question

Is $\mathsf{BPP}$ really the right notion of “efficiently computable”?

Guiding Question

Where does quantum computation sit relative to $\mathsf{P}$, $\mathsf{BPP}$ and $\mathsf{PSPACE}$?

The thesis that CS455 quietly relies on

Every time CS455 says "$\mathsf{P}$ is the class of efficiently solvable problems," it is making a claim about the physical world, not just about Turing machines. The claim is called the Extended (or Strong) Church–Turing Thesis:

Extended Church–Turing Thesis (ECT). Any "physically reasonable" model of computation can be simulated by a (probabilistic) Turing machine with at most polynomial overhead in time.

The ordinary Church–Turing thesis is about what can be computed. The extended version is about how fast. It is why we can be casual about the model in CS455: single-tape or multi-tape TMs, RAM machines, Boolean circuits, Python programs. They all simulate each other with polynomial slowdown, so $\mathsf{P}$ and $\mathsf{BPP}$ come out the same.

Notice that the thesis says "probabilistic." Randomness was the first serious candidate for a resource that might break the "deterministic" version of the thesis, and CS455 handles it by defining $\mathsf{BPP}$ and asking whether $\mathsf{P} = \mathsf{BPP}$. (Most complexity theorists now believe they are equal, because of derandomization results.)

Quantum mechanics challenges the thesis

Physics, as far as we know, is quantum mechanical. In 1982 Richard Feynman pointed out that simulating a quantum system of $m$ particles on a classical computer seems to require keeping track of about $2^m$ numbers (we will see why in the Qubits and Quantum States chapter), and asked the obvious question: if nature is "doing" that computation for free, can we harness it? This led to a sequence of results that every complexity theorist knows:

  • 1985, Deutsch: a formal model of a quantum Turing machine.
  • 1993, Bernstein–Vazirani: the class $\mathsf{BQP}$ ("bounded-error quantum polynomial time"), the quantum analogue of $\mathsf{BPP}$. They also proved the first simulation results, and gave an oracle problem that quantum computers solve much faster than classical ones.
  • 1994, Simon: an oracle problem with an exponential quantum speed-up over randomized algorithms.
  • 1994, Shor: polynomial-time quantum algorithms for factoring integers and computing discrete logarithms.

Shor's algorithm is the reason this matters outside theory. Factoring is not known to be in $\mathsf{BPP}$; the best known classical algorithms take time roughly $2^{\tilde O(n^{1/3})}$ on $n$-bit numbers. The security of RSA rests on factoring being hard. So if large quantum computers can be built and factoring is not in $\mathsf{BPP}$, then the Extended Church–Turing Thesis is false, and "efficiently computable" means $\mathsf{BQP}$, not $\mathsf{BPP}$.

Remark (A common misconception)

Quantum computers do not work by "trying all answers in parallel." If they did, they would solve $\mathsf{NP}$-complete problems easily, and there is good evidence that they can't (see the known separations). A quantum state can indeed involve exponentially many classical strings at once, but a measurement shows you just one of them, at random. The real resource is subtler: quantum algorithms arrange for the "wrong" computation paths to cancel each other out. These notes is largely about making that one sentence precise.

Where this fits in CS455

In CS455 you built up the map of classes $\mathsf{L} \subseteq \mathsf{NL} \subseteq \mathsf{P} \subseteq \mathsf{NP} \subseteq \mathsf{PSPACE} \subseteq \mathsf{EXP}$, and added randomness to get $\mathsf{P} \subseteq \mathsf{BPP} \subseteq \mathsf{PSPACE}$. The two questions we answer

Complexity theory pins down a new class by proving inclusions from both sides. For $\mathsf{BQP}$:

How powerful at least?

Lower bounds on power: $\mathsf{P}\subseteq\mathsf{BPP}\subseteq\mathsf{BQP}$ (the BQP and Reversible Computation chapter). So quantum computers are no weaker than classical ones. That isn't obvious, because quantum operations must be reversible! Then evidence that they are strictly stronger: query separations (the Quantum Query Algorithms chapter).

How powerful at most?

Upper bounds: $\mathsf{BQP}\subseteq\mathsf{PSPACE}$ (the BQP ⊆ PSPACE chapter) and the sharper $\mathsf{BQP}\subseteq\mathsf{PP}$ (the Main Theorem chapter). So quantum computers do not violate the ordinary Church–Turing thesis, and they can be simulated by a classical machine that can count.

The upper bounds matter just as much as the algorithms. They tell us that quantum computers cannot be too powerful, and they explain why nobody has proved that quantum computers beat classical ones: by the Main Theorem, such a proof would also show $\mathsf{P} \neq \mathsf{PP}$, and that would be a major breakthrough in classical complexity theory (see the Consequences Corollary).

What these notes do not do

To keep the focus on complexity theory, we leave out three things, and say so whenever they come up:

  • Physics. We take the mathematical rules (states are unit vectors, operations are unitary, measurement follows the Born rule) as axioms.
  • Shor's algorithm. It needs the quantum Fourier transform and some number theory, more than two lectures on its own. We state it (see fact) and give references.
  • Gate-set approximation (Solovay–Kitaev). We fix one convenient finite gate set and cite the theorem that says this choice doesn't matter (see theorem).

Summary

  • "$\mathsf{P}$ (or $\mathsf{BPP}$) = efficient" depends on the Extended Church–Turing Thesis, and quantum computing is the most serious known challenge to it.
  • $\mathsf{BQP}$ is to quantum circuits what $\mathsf{BPP}$ is to coin-flipping machines.
  • The goal is to sandwich $\mathsf{BQP}$: $\mathsf{BPP}\subseteq\mathsf{BQP}\subseteq\mathsf{PP}\subseteq\mathsf{PSPACE}$.
  • The power of quantum computing comes from cancellation (interference), not from "parallel search."
