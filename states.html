Qubits and Quantum States

In this chapter we describe the memory of a quantum computer. Recall from Probabilistic Time Complexity 1 that a randomized computation can be described by a probability distribution over its configurations. A quantum computation is described by something similar: a vector of amplitudes. We define these vectors and explain what we can learn from them. The next chapter covers the operations we can apply to them.

From bits to qubits

A random bit that is $0$ with probability $p_0$ and $1$ with probability $p_1$ can be written as a vector $$\begin{pmatrix}p_0\\p_1\end{pmatrix},\qquad p_0,p_1\ge 0,\quad p_0+p_1 = 1.$$ A normal (deterministic) bit is the special case $(1,0)^T$ or $(0,1)^T$. A qubit is what we get if we allow the entries to be any numbers, including negative or complex ones, and require the squares of their magnitudes to add up to $1$ instead of the entries themselves.

Definition (Qubit; Dirac notation)

A qubit is a unit vector in $\C^2$. We name the two standard basis vectors $$\ket 0 = \begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \ket 1 = \begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix},$$ so a general qubit is $$\ket\psi = \alpha\ket0 + \beta\ket1 = \begin{pmatrix}\alpha\\\beta\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \alpha,\beta\in\C,\quad |\alpha|^2+|\beta|^2 = 1.$$ The numbers $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are called amplitudes. The notation $\ket{\cdot}$ (a “ket”) just means “the column vector named $\cdot$”.

We can never see the amplitudes directly. The only way to get information out of a qubit is to measure it:

Definition (Measuring a qubit: the Born rule)

Measuring the qubit $\alpha\ket0+\beta\ket1$ gives $0$ with probability $|\alpha|^2$ and $1$ with probability $|\beta|^2$. Afterwards the qubit is in state $\ket 0$ or $\ket 1$, matching the outcome.

Example ($\ket+$ and $\ket-$)

Two qubit states will come up a lot: $$\ket+ = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\ket0 + \tfrac1{\sqrt2}\ket1, \qquad \ket- = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}\ket0 - \tfrac1{\sqrt2}\ket1.$$ Measuring either one gives $0$ or $1$ with probability $\big(\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\big)^2 = \tfrac12$ each, so if we measure right away they both look like fair coins. But they are different states. In the next chapter we will apply a gate ($H$) that turns $\ket+$ into $\ket0$ and $\ket-$ into $\ket1$, so the difference becomes visible. The sign of an amplitude doesn’t affect a measurement right now, but it does change what happens later.

Many qubits

A classical $m$-bit register holds one string $z\in\{0,1\}^m$. A random register is a probability distribution over the $2^m$ strings. A quantum register is a vector of amplitudes over the $2^m$ strings:

Definition ($m$-qubit state; amplitude notation)

An $m$-qubit state is a unit vector in $\C^{2^m}$ whose coordinates are indexed by strings $z\in\{0,1\}^m$ (in lexicographic order). The standard basis vector for $z$ is written $\ket z$ and is called a basis state. A general state is $$\ket\psi = \sum_{z\in\{0,1\}^m}\alpha_z\ket z,\qquad \sum_z|\alpha_z|^2 = 1.$$ We write $\braket{z}{\psi} := \alpha_z$ for the amplitude of $\ket z$ in $\ket\psi$. We number the qubits $1,\dots,m$ from left to right, and say that in the basis state $\ket z$, qubit $j$ holds the bit $z_j$.

(Formally, $\bra z$ is the row vector $\ket z^\dagger$, so $\braket z\psi$ is an inner product. In these notes you can just read it as “the $z$-th coordinate”.)

This connects to single qubits through the tensor product (see definition). The tensor product of basis vectors is a basis vector, in lexicographic order (see fact), so:

Fact (Side by side = tensor product)

$\ket{z_1}\otimes\ket{z_2}\otimes\cdots\otimes\ket{z_m} = \ket{z_1z_2\cdots z_m}$. More generally, if qubits $1$ to $k$ are in state $\ket\psi$ and qubits $k{+}1$ to $m$ are separately in state $\ket\phi$, then the whole register is in state $\ket\psi\otimes\ket\phi$. We often leave out the $\otimes$ and write $\ket\psi\ket\phi$. For example, $\ket x\ket{0^{m-n}} = \ket{x0^{m-n}}$.

Definition (Measuring several qubits, or just one)

Let $\ket\psi = \sum_z \alpha_z\ket z$.

  • Measuring all $m$ qubits gives outcome $z$ with probability $|\alpha_z|^2$.
  • Measuring only qubit $j$ gives $1$ with probability $$\Pr[\text{qubit } j \text{ reads } 1] = \sum_{z\,:\,z_j = 1}|\alpha_z|^2,$$ and $0$ otherwise. (We will not need the state after a measurement, because we only ever measure at the very end of a computation.)

These two rules agree: measuring only qubit $j$ gives $1$ with the same probability as measuring everything and looking at bit $j$.

Example (Measuring one qubit at a time)

Let $\ket\psi = \tfrac1{\sqrt3}\big(\ket{00}+\ket{01}+\ket{11}\big)$. Measuring both qubits gives $00$, $01$ or $11$, each with probability $\tfrac13$, and never $10$. Measuring only qubit 1 gives $1$ with probability $|\alpha_{10}|^2+|\alpha_{11}|^2 = 0+\tfrac13 = \tfrac13$. Measuring only qubit 2 gives $1$ with probability $|\alpha_{01}|^2+|\alpha_{11}|^2 = \tfrac23$.

Entanglement

Definition (Product states and entanglement)

A two-qubit state $\ket\psi$ is a product state if $\ket\psi = \ket a\otimes\ket b$ for some single-qubit states $\ket a$ and $\ket b$. Otherwise it is entangled.

Example (The Bell state is entangled)

The Bell state is $$\ket{\Phi^+} = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}\big(\ket{00}+\ket{11}\big).$$

The idea. In a product state $\ket a\otimes\ket b$, each qubit has a state of its own, so what happens to one qubit has nothing to do with the other. The Bell state is different. If we measure both qubits, we get $00$ or $11$, each with probability $\tfrac12$, and never $01$ or $10$. So each bit on its own is a fair coin flip, but the two bits always agree. Once you know qubit 1, you know qubit 2. Two separate qubits, each with its own state, can’t do this, so the Bell state can’t be split into a state for qubit 1 and a state for qubit 2.

The proof. Suppose $\ket{\Phi^+}$ were a product state $(a_0\ket0+a_1\ket1)\otimes(b_0\ket0+b_1\ket1)$. Multiplying this out gives $$a_0b_0\ket{00}+a_0b_1\ket{01}+a_1b_0\ket{10}+a_1b_1\ket{11}.$$ For this to equal $\ket{\Phi^+}$, the amplitudes have to match:

  • The amplitude of $\ket{00}$ is $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}$, so $a_0b_0\ne0$. In particular $a_0\ne0$.
  • The amplitude of $\ket{11}$ is $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}$, so $a_1b_1\ne0$. In particular $b_1\ne0$.
  • The amplitude of $\ket{01}$ is $0$, so $a_0b_1 = 0$.

But the first two bullets say $a_0\ne0$ and $b_1\ne0$, so $a_0b_1\ne 0$. That contradicts the third bullet. So no choice of $a_0,a_1,b_0,b_1$ works, and $\ket{\Phi^+}$ is entangled.

Compare with $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\big(\ket{00}+\ket{01}\big)$, which looks similar but is the product state $\ket0\otimes\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket0+\ket1)$. Here qubit 1 is always $0$ and qubit 2 is a fair coin, and the two have nothing to do with each other.

We build $\ket{\Phi^+}$ with two gates in the next chapter. In the which-path example we will see that this kind of link between qubits is exactly what stops paths from cancelling, and that is why entanglement with leftover “garbage” is a problem.

Exponentially many amplitudes

Remark ($2^m$ numbers, but only $m$ bits out)

A product state of $m$ qubits can be described with $2m$ amplitudes, but a general (entangled) state needs all $2^m$. For $m=300$, that is more numbers than there are atoms in the observable universe. This is Feynman’s observation: writing down a quantum state on a normal computer seems to take exponential resources. There are two catches. First, a measurement only gives us a single $m$-bit string (Holevo’s theorem says $m$ qubits carry at most $m$ bits of classical information; see Nielsen–Chuang §12.1). Second, in the Main Theorem chapter we will compute measurement probabilities without writing down all $2^m$ amplitudes.

Summary

  • An $m$-qubit state is a unit vector of $2^m$ amplitudes $\braket z\psi$, one for each string $z$.
  • Measuring turns amplitudes into probabilities by squaring their magnitudes: $\Pr[z] = |\braket z\psi|^2$.
  • Signs don’t affect a measurement right away ($\ket+$ and $\ket-$ look the same), but they change what happens later.
