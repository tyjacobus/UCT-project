Overview and roadmap

Quantum Computation: A Complexity Theory View

How powerful are quantum computers? We define the class $\mathsf{BQP}$ carefully and then trap it between two classical classes: $\mathsf{BPP} \subseteq \mathsf{BQP} \subseteq \mathsf{PP}$.

The story in one paragraph

A randomized algorithm can be described by assigning a probability to every possible computation path; the probability of an outcome is the sum over the paths that lead to it. A quantum algorithm does the same thing with amplitudes, which are allowed to be negative. Positive and negative contributions can cancel, and that cancellation (called interference) is the only new ingredient. It is enough to give quantum computers a fast algorithm for factoring, which nobody knows how to do classically. On the other hand, a sum over paths with signs is exactly the kind of thing a classical counting machine can evaluate. Making that precise proves this site's main theorem:

Centerpiece theorem (Adleman, DeMarrais and Huang, 1997)

$\mathsf{BQP} \subseteq \mathsf{PP}$. Every language decided by a polynomial-time quantum algorithm with bounded error is decided by a polynomial-time probabilistic Turing machine that accepts with probability more than $1/2$ exactly on members of the language.

Stated precisely as Theorem 9.1 and proved in full on Page 9.

What you will learn

By the end of the site you should be able to:

  1. Compute with qubits, unitary gates and measurements, and explain exactly how a quantum circuit differs from a randomized one. The difference is signs, and the cancellation they allow.
  2. State the definition of $\mathsf{BQP}$ precisely and prove $\mathsf{P} \subseteq \mathsf{BPP} \subseteq \mathsf{BQP}$, using reversible computation and Bennett's "uncompute" trick.
  3. Analyze the Deutsch–Jozsa and Bernstein–Vazirani algorithms, and say what kind of evidence they give that $\mathsf{BQP}$ is more powerful than $\mathsf{BPP}$.
  4. Prove that amplitudes are signed counts of paths (the path-sum lemma), and use it to show $\mathsf{BQP} \subseteq \mathsf{PSPACE}$.
  5. Prove the centerpiece theorem $\mathsf{BQP} \subseteq \mathsf{PP}$, and explain why the proof needs pairs of paths.

Roadmap

The pages are meant to be read in order. Together they are about two 455 lectures' worth of material. Lecture A (Pages 3–6) builds the model and ends with the definition of $\mathsf{BQP}$ and its first inclusions. Lecture B (Pages 7–9) asks how powerful the model is, from below (algorithms) and from above (simulations), and ends with the centerpiece proof.

  1. 1.Why Quantum Complexity?

    The Extended Church–Turing thesis, where quantum computing sits relative to the classes you know from 455, and why complexity theorists care.

  2. 2.Background Review

    Exactly what we assume from 455, plus short reviews of what goes beyond it: complex vectors and unitary matrices, tensor products, the class $\mathsf{PP}$, and uniform circuit families. Skim it now, and come back when a later page links here.

    Key items: Definition 2.5 ($\mathsf{PP}$), Proposition 2.6 ($\mathsf{PP}\subseteq\mathsf{PSPACE}$), Fact 2.8 (TMs → circuits)

  3. 3.Qubits and Quantum States Lecture A

    Qubits as unit vectors, Dirac notation, measurement, multi-qubit states via tensor products, entanglement (the Bell state), and why $m$ qubits need $2^m$ numbers to describe.

  4. 4.Unitary Gates and Circuits Lecture A

    Why operations must be unitary; the gates $X$, $Z$, $S$, $H$, CNOT and Toffoli with worked examples; building a Bell state; the formal definition of a quantum circuit; and the Hadamard transform formula that every later page uses.

    Key items: Lemma 4.4 (Hadamard formula), Definition 4.10 (quantum circuit), Definition 4.11 (our gate set $\mathcal G$)

  5. 5.Quantum vs. Randomized Computation Lecture A

    Randomized computation as stochastic matrices; quantum as unitary matrices. Flipping a coin twice versus applying $H$ twice. The sum-over-paths picture, and two ways interference can be destroyed (measuring, and leaving "which-path" garbage).

    Key item: Lemma 5.3 (sum over paths), which is the seed of the centerpiece proof

  6. 6.BQP and Reversible Computation Lecture A

    The formal definition of $\mathsf{BQP}$. Then reversible computing with Toffoli gates, garbage, and Bennett's uncomputation trick, giving $\mathsf{P}\subseteq\mathsf{BQP}$ and $\mathsf{BPP}\subseteq\mathsf{BQP}$.

    Key items: Definition 6.2 ($\mathsf{BQP}$), Lemma 6.5 (clean computation), Theorem 6.7 ($\mathsf{BPP}\subseteq\mathsf{BQP}$)

  7. 7.Evidence of Power: Query Algorithms Lecture B

    Oracles and phase kickback; the Deutsch–Jozsa and Bernstein–Vazirani algorithms with matching classical lower bounds; a guided tour (without proofs) of Simon, Shor, Grover and the oracle separations.

  8. 8.Warm-up: BQP ⊆ PSPACE Lecture B

    Schrödinger-style simulation ($\mathsf{BQP}\subseteq\mathsf{EXP}$) versus Feynman-style simulation. The path-sum lemma: every amplitude is $2^{-h/2}$ times a signed count of paths. Then $\mathsf{BQP}\subseteq\mathsf{PSPACE}$ by reusing space.

    Key items: Lemma 8.3 (path-sum lemma), Theorem 8.6 ($\mathsf{BQP}\subseteq\mathsf{PSPACE}$)

  9. 9.Centerpiece: BQP ⊆ PP ★ Lecture B

    Why squaring amplitudes is the obstacle, and how pairs of paths get around it; the "abstain" trick for signed counting in $\mathsf{PP}$; the full proof, with an exact formula for the $\mathsf{PP}$ machine's acceptance probability; and an interactive explorer.

    Key items: Lemma 9.2 (signed counting is in $\mathsf{PP}$), Lemma 9.3 (two-path formula), Theorem 9.1

  10. 10.Conclusion and References

    What we know and what is open (is $\mathsf{BPP} \ne \mathsf{BQP}$? is $\mathsf{NP}\subseteq\mathsf{BQP}$?), why proving quantum advantage unconditionally would separate $\mathsf{P}$ from $\mathsf{PSPACE}$, a list of every omitted detail with its source, and all references.

Prerequisites

This site assumes exactly the background of a 15-455 student:

  • Turing machines, running time and space; the classes $\mathsf{P}$, $\mathsf{NP}$, $\mathsf{PSPACE}$, $\mathsf{EXP}$; the idea of reusing space (as in Savitch's theorem).
  • Probabilistic Turing machines and $\mathsf{BPP}$ (including error reduction by repetition).
  • Boolean circuits, and the fact (used in the Cook–Levin theorem) that a polynomial-time machine can be turned into polynomial-size circuits by an efficient procedure.
  • Basic linear algebra (vectors, matrices, matrix multiplication) and discrete probability.

Everything beyond that is reviewed on Page 2: complex numbers and unitary matrices, tensor products, the class $\mathsf{PP}$ (which 455 may not cover), and uniform circuit families. No physics is assumed. We treat quantum mechanics as a mathematical model of computation, the way 455 treats coin flips.
