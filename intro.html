Why Quantum Complexity?

In this chapter we explain why quantum computation matters to complexity theory and how it fits with the classes we have studied in CS455.

The Extended Church–Turing thesis

In the Introduction to CS455 we saw that the standard models of computation can all simulate each other with at most a polynomial slowdown. This is why classes like $\mathsf{P}$ and $\mathsf{BPP}$ do not depend on which model we pick. Many people believe this is true for every model that could actually be built:

Extended Church–Turing Thesis. Any physically reasonable model of computation can be simulated by a probabilistic TM with at most polynomial overhead in time.

If this thesis is true, then $\mathsf{BPP}$ is the right definition of “efficiently solvable”. Quantum computation is the biggest known challenge to it.

Why quantum computation challenges the thesis

In 1982 Feynman noticed that simulating a quantum system of $m$ particles on a normal computer seems to need about $2^m$ numbers (we will see why in the Qubits and Quantum States chapter). He asked whether we could use quantum systems to compute faster. In 1993 Bernstein and Vazirani defined $\mathsf{BQP}$ (bounded-error quantum polynomial time), the quantum version of $\mathsf{BPP}$. There is good evidence that $\mathsf{BQP}$ is more powerful than $\mathsf{BPP}$, but also evidence that it is not all-powerful:

Fact (Evidence for and against quantum power)
  1. Shor (1994). Quantum computers can factor integers and compute discrete logarithms in polynomial time. No efficient classical algorithm for factoring is known (the best ones take about $2^{\tilde O(n^{1/3})}$ time), and the security of RSA depends on factoring being hard.
  2. Simon (1994). There is an oracle $A$ with $\mathsf{BPP}^A\ne\mathsf{BQP}^A$: a quantum algorithm needs $O(n)$ queries where every randomized algorithm needs $\Omega(2^{n/2})$. Raz and Tal (2018) even found an oracle with $\mathsf{BQP}^A\not\subseteq\mathsf{PH}^A$.
  3. Grover (1996) and Bennett, Bernstein, Brassard and Vazirani (1997). Searching $N$ unsorted items takes $\Theta(\sqrt N)$ quantum queries, which is only a quadratic speed-up. Relative to a random oracle, $\mathsf{NP}\not\subseteq\mathsf{BQP}$. So quantum computers probably cannot solve $\mathsf{NP}$-complete problems efficiently.
Remark (Omitted details)

We do not prove these results. As we saw in the Relativization chapter, oracle separations are evidence, not proofs. For Shor’s and Simon’s algorithms see Arora–Barak, Chapter 10, and Nielsen–Chuang, Chapter 5. For Grover’s algorithm and its matching lower bound see Nielsen–Chuang, Chapter 6. The original papers are listed in the References.

So if large quantum computers can be built and factoring is not in $\mathsf{BPP}$, the Extended Church–Turing thesis is false. The third item also shows that quantum computers do not work by trying every answer in parallel. A quantum state can involve exponentially many strings at once, but measuring it only shows you one of them, at random. What quantum algorithms actually do is arrange for the computation paths that lead to wrong answers to cancel out. Most of these notes are about making that idea precise.

What we will prove

Like every new class in CS455, we place $\mathsf{BQP}$ by proving inclusions from both sides:

  • From below: $\mathsf{P}\subseteq\mathsf{BPP}\subseteq\mathsf{BQP}$ (in the BQP and Reversible Computation chapter). This takes some work, because quantum operations have to be reversible.
  • From above: $\mathsf{BQP}\subseteq\mathsf{PSPACE}$ (in the BQP ⊆ PSPACE chapter), and then the stronger $\mathsf{BQP}\subseteq\mathsf{PP}$ (in the Main Theorem chapter). This means a quantum computer can be simulated by a classical machine that can count.

The upper bound also explains why nobody has proved that quantum computers are more powerful than classical ones. By the Main Theorem, such a proof would show $\mathsf{P}\neq\mathsf{PP}$, and therefore $\mathsf{P}\ne\mathsf{PSPACE}$ (see the Consequences Corollary). We treat the rules of quantum mechanics as definitions, so no physics is needed, and we leave out quantum algorithms like Shor’s.

Summary

  • Calling $\mathsf{BPP}$ “efficient” relies on the Extended Church–Turing thesis, and quantum computation is the biggest known challenge to it.
  • $\mathsf{BQP}$ is the quantum version of $\mathsf{BPP}$. Our goal is to show $\mathsf{BPP}\subseteq\mathsf{BQP}\subseteq\mathsf{PP}\subseteq\mathsf{PSPACE}$.
  • Quantum speed-ups come from paths cancelling out, not from trying every answer in parallel.
