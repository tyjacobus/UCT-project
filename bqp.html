$\mathsf{BQP}$ and Reversible Computation

We now have all the pieces to define the class $\mathsf{BQP}$, the quantum analog of $\mathsf{BPP}$. In this chapter we define it, and then prove the lower end of our sandwich: $\mathsf{P}\subseteq\mathsf{BPP}\subseteq\mathsf{BQP}$. The main obstacle is that quantum computation must be reversible, while ordinary Boolean circuits are not.

Guiding Question

How do we define efficient quantum computation?

Guiding Question

Can a quantum computer simulate a classical one, even though AND is not reversible?

The class BQP

A quantum algorithm takes a classical input $x\in\{0,1\}^n$, loads it into the first $n$ qubits, sets every other qubit ("ancilla") to $\ket0$, runs a circuit, and measures one qubit to get its answer. We use the last qubit as the answer qubit, so that it never clashes with the input.

Definition (Acceptance probability)

Let $C$ be a circuit on $m$ qubits and $x\in\{0,1\}^n$ with $n\le m$. Running $C$ on $x$ means preparing $\ket{x0^{m-n}}$, applying $C$, and measuring qubit $m$. $C$ accepts if the outcome is $1$. By the measurement rule, the acceptance probability is $$p_C(x) \;=\; \sum_{y\in\{0,1\}^m:\;y_m = 1}\big|\amp{y}{C}{x0^{m-n}}\big|^2 .$$

Definition ($\mathsf{BQP}$, bounded-error quantum polynomial time; Bernstein–Vazirani 1993)

A quantum circuit family $\mathcal C = (C_0, C_1, C_2,\dots)$ solves a language $L$ with error $\epsilon$ if $$\forall x\in L,\quad \Pr[C_{|x|}(x)\text{ accepts}]\ge 1-\epsilon, \qquad\qquad \forall x\notin L,\quad \Pr[C_{|x|}(x)\text{ accepts}]\le\epsilon,$$ where $\Pr[C(x)\text{ accepts}] = p_C(x)$ is the acceptance probability defined above. Then $$\mathsf{BQP} = \{L \mid \exists \text{ polynomial-time uniform circuit family } \mathcal C \text{ over } \mathcal G \text{ solving } L \text{ with error } \tfrac13\}.$$ Here $\mathcal G = \{H,X,\CNOT,\TOF\}$ is our gate set (see definition), and polynomial-time uniform means, just as for Boolean circuits (see definition), that a deterministic polynomial-time TM on input $1^n$ outputs the list of gates of $C_n$ and the qubits each one acts on. In particular $C_n$ has $m(n)$ qubits and $T(n)$ gates, both polynomial in $n$.

Put this next to the definition of $\mathsf{BPP}$. The definition is word for word the same, including the error $\tfrac13$. The only change is that the PTM is replaced by a uniform family of quantum circuits.

Remark (The definition is robust)
  1. Gate set. By the universality theorem for Hadamard + Toffoli, replacing $\mathcal G$ by any standard universal gate set gives the same class. (Simulate with error $\varepsilon=0.01$; then $\tfrac23$ and $\tfrac13$ become $0.656$ and $0.343$, and part 2 restores them.)
  2. Error. The constants $\tfrac23,\tfrac13$ can be replaced by $1-2^{-q(n)}$ and $2^{-q(n)}$ for any polynomial $q$, exactly as for $\mathsf{BPP}$. Run $k = O(q(n))$ copies of $C_n$ on disjoint sets of qubits. Their answer qubits, if measured, would give $k$ independent outcomes, because the joint state is a tensor product. So compute the majority of the answer qubits into a fresh qubit with a reversible circuit (Bennett's Uncomputation Lemma below) and measure that. Hoeffding’s inequality finishes the argument exactly as in the Two-sided Error Reduction theorem of Probabilistic Time Complexity 2.
  3. Measurement. Measuring a single qubit, once, at the end, is no loss of generality, by the principle of deferred measurement (the Unitary Gates and Circuits chapter).
  4. Uniformity matters. Without it, the family $\{C_n\}$ could encode an undecidable language in which circuit is chosen for each $n$, just as with $\mathsf{P/poly}$.
Remark (Omitted details)

Item 1 depends on the universality theorem for Hadamard + Toffoli, whose proof we omit (sources there). Item 2 is only sketched; for a full treatment see Bernstein–Vazirani, "Quantum complexity theory," SIAM J. Comput. 26(5), 1997, or Arora–Barak, Chapter 10. Item 3 is Nielsen–Chuang, §4.4. None of these are used in the proofs of $\mathsf{P}\subseteq\mathsf{BQP}$, $\mathsf{BPP}\subseteq\mathsf{BQP}$, $\mathsf{BQP}\subseteq\mathsf{PSPACE}$ or the Main Theorem, which work directly with the definition of $\mathsf{BQP}$ as stated.

Making classical circuits reversible

To prove $\mathsf{P}\subseteq\mathsf{BQP}$, we want to run an ordinary Boolean circuit $D$ (with AND, OR, NOT gates) on a quantum computer. The obstacle (see remark) is that AND and OR are not reversible. The fix is to never overwrite anything: every gate writes its output into a fresh qubit, initially $\ket0$, and the Toffoli gate computes AND that way: $\TOF\ket{a,b,0} = \ket{a,b,a\wedge b}$.

Lemma (Reversible Simulation Lemma)

Let $D$ be a Boolean circuit with $n$ inputs and $s$ gates (AND, OR, NOT; fan-in at most 2; any fan-out), whose gates in topological order compute the bits $g_1(x),\dots,g_s(x)$. There is a circuit $R_D$ over $\{X,\CNOT,\TOF\}$ on $n+s$ qubits with at most $6s$ gates such that for every $x\in\{0,1\}^n$, $$R_D\ket{x}\ket{0^s} = \ket{x}\ket{g(x)},\qquad\text{where } g(x) = g_1(x)\,g_2(x)\cdots g_s(x).$$ Moreover, $R_D$ can be computed from $D$ in polynomial time.

Proof. (A binary gate whose two inputs are the same wire $a$ just computes $a$; handle it with a single $\CNOT$ from $a$ into its fresh qubit. So assume below that binary gates read two different wires.) Give gate $i$ of $D$ its own qubit $n+i$, starting in $\ket0$. Handle the gates in topological order. When we reach gate $i$, its input wires (input bits or earlier gate qubits) already hold their correct values. Then:
  • $\mathrm{NOT}(a)$: apply $\CNOT$ from $a$ to qubit $n+i$ (now it holds $a$), then $X$ on qubit $n+i$ (now it holds $\neg a$).
  • $\mathrm{AND}(a,b)$: apply $\TOF$ with controls $a,b$ and target $n+i$, which now holds $0\oplus(a\wedge b) = a\wedge b$.
  • $\mathrm{OR}(a,b) = \neg(\neg a\wedge\neg b)$: apply $X$ to $a$ and $b$, then $\TOF$ into $n+i$, then $X$ to $a$ and $b$ again (restoring them), then $X$ on $n+i$. That is $6$ gates.
Each step changes only qubit $n+i$ permanently, and every wire it reads is unchanged afterwards. So by induction on $i$, after handling gate $i$ the register holds $x,\,g_1(x),\dots,g_i(x),\,0,\dots,0$. Fan-out costs nothing, because a wire can be used as a control any number of times. All gates are classical, so this computation on basis states is exactly the classical one. ∎

On a single basis state, the Reversible Simulation Lemma is all we need. But quantum algorithms apply circuits to superpositions, and then the extra bits $g(x)$, the "garbage," cause trouble. By linearity, $$R_D\Big(\sum_x\alpha_x\ket x\Big)\ket{0^s} = \sum_x\alpha_x\ket x\ket{g(x)} .$$ The garbage register is now entangled with $x$. It is a which-path record, and by the interference principle it prevents different $x$'s from interfering later on. Charles Bennett's 1973 trick removes it: compute, copy the answer, then run the computation backwards.

Lemma (Bennett's Uncomputation Lemma)

Let $D$ be a Boolean circuit with $n$ inputs and $s$ gates computing $f:\{0,1\}^n\to\{0,1\}$ (the output is the last gate, $f = g_s$). There is a circuit $B_D$ over $\{X,\CNOT,\TOF\}$ on $n+s+1$ qubits, with at most $12s+1$ gates and computable from $D$ in polynomial time, such that for all $x\in\{0,1\}^n$ and $b\in\{0,1\}$: $$B_D\,\ket{x}\ket{0^s}\ket{b} \;=\; \ket{x}\ket{0^s}\ket{b\oplus f(x)} .$$

Proof. Let $B_D = R_D^{-1}\cdot \CNOT_{[n+s,\;n+s+1]}\cdot R_D$, where $R_D$ is from the Reversible Simulation Lemma and $R_D^{-1}$ is the gates of $R_D$ in reverse order. (Each of $X$, CNOT, TOF is its own inverse, so this really is the inverse.) Follow a basis state through the three stages: $$\ket x\ket{0^s}\ket b\;\xrightarrow{\;R_D\;}\;\ket x\ket{g(x)}\ket b\;\xrightarrow{\;\CNOT\;}\;\ket x\ket{g(x)}\ket{b\oplus f(x)}\;\xrightarrow{\;R_D^{-1}\;}\;\ket x\ket{0^s}\ket{b\oplus f(x)}.$$ The first arrow is the Reversible Simulation Lemma (and $R_D$ does not touch the last qubit). The second copies the last scratch bit, $g_s(x) = f(x)$, into the last qubit. For the third: $R_D^{-1}$ acts only on the first $n+s$ qubits, and it sends $\ket x\ket{g(x)}$ back to $\ket x\ket{0^s}$, because $R_D$ sends $\ket x\ket{0^s}$ to $\ket x\ket{g(x)}$. The gate count is $6s+1+6s$. ∎
Remark (Why this works)

Extend Bennett's Uncomputation Lemma linearly to a superposition: $$B_D\sum_{x,b}\alpha_{x,b}\ket x\ket{0^s}\ket b = \sum_{x,b}\alpha_{x,b}\ket x\ket{0^s}\ket{b\oplus f(x)} .$$ The scratch register is $\ket{0^s}$ in every branch, so it factors out as a tensor product and records nothing. The circuit acts on the registers we care about as the clean, reversible map $\ket x\ket b\mapsto\ket x\ket{b\oplus f(x)}$, and branches can still interfere afterwards. We could not have simply "reset" the garbage instead, because resetting a bit to $0$ is not reversible. Measuring it doesn't help either: a measurement outcome is itself a which-path record.

P ⊆ BQP and BPP ⊆ BQP

Theorem ($\mathsf{P}\subseteq\mathsf{BQP}$)

Every language in $\mathsf{P}$ is in $\mathsf{BQP}$. In fact, the circuits accept with probability exactly $1$ or $0$.

Proof. Let $L\in\mathsf{P}$. By the Efficient TM to Efficient Circuit theorem there is a polynomial-time uniform family of Boolean circuits $D_n$, of size $s(n) = \mathrm{poly}(n)$, deciding $L$ on inputs of length $n$. Let $C_n = B_{D_n}$ from Bennett's Uncomputation Lemma, on $m = n+s+1$ qubits. $C_n$ uses only gates from $\mathcal G$, and it can be printed in polynomial time: first print $D_n$, then apply the polynomial-time transformation of the Reversible Simulation Lemma and Bennett\'s Uncomputation Lemma. Running $C_n$ on $x$ starts from $\ket{x}\ket{0^s}\ket0$ and ends in the basis state $\ket{x}\ket{0^s}\ket{L(x)}$, where $L(x) = 1$ if $x\in L$ and $0$ otherwise. So $p_{C_n}(x) = 1$ if $x\in L$ and $0$ otherwise. ∎

For $\mathsf{BPP}$ we also need coin flips. As previewed in the last section of the previous chapter, a Hadamard gate on a fresh qubit gives a coin, provided we never let its branches interfere.

Theorem ($\mathsf{BPP}\subseteq\mathsf{BQP}$)

Every language in $\mathsf{BPP}$ is in $\mathsf{BQP}$. More precisely, for every polynomial-time PTM $M$ there is a polynomial-time uniform circuit family $\mathcal C = (C_0, C_1, \dots)$ over $\mathcal G$ with $p_{C_n}(x) = \Pr[M(x)\text{ accepts}]$ for every $x$.

Proof. Use the random-tape view (see the Background Review): there is a polynomial $r(n)$ and a deterministic polynomial-time TM $V(x,\rho)$, with $\rho\in\{0,1\}^{r(|x|)}$, such that $\Pr[M(x)\text{ accepts}] = \Pr_{\rho}[V(x,\rho)\text{ accepts}]$. Write $r = r(n)$. By the Efficient TM to Efficient Circuit theorem applied to $V$ on inputs of length $n+r$, there are uniform Boolean circuits $D_n$ of size $s = \mathrm{poly}(n)$ with $D_n(x,\rho) = V(x,\rho)$.

Lay out $m = n + r + s + 1$ qubits as four registers: input $x$ ($n$ qubits), coins ($r$), scratch ($s$), answer ($1$). Let $C_n$ be:

  1. apply $H$ to each of the $r$ coin qubits;
  2. apply $B_{D_n}$ (Bennett's Uncomputation Lemma), treating "input + coins" as its $n+r$ input qubits.

Track the state. Initially it is $\ket x\ket{0^r}\ket{0^s}\ket0$. By the Hadamard Transform Lemma with $x=0^r$, step 1 gives $$\frac{1}{2^{r/2}}\sum_{\rho\in\{0,1\}^r}\ket x\ket\rho\ket{0^s}\ket0 .$$ By Bennett's Uncomputation Lemma and linearity, step 2 gives $$\frac{1}{2^{r/2}}\sum_{\rho\in\{0,1\}^r}\ket x\ket\rho\ket{0^s}\ket{V(x,\rho)} .$$ Different $\rho$ give different basis states (they differ in the coin register), so each basis state $\ket{x\,\rho\,0^s\,V(x,\rho)}$ has amplitude exactly $2^{-r/2}$, and no two terms combine. By the definition of acceptance probability, $$p_{C_n}(x) = \sum_{\rho:\,V(x,\rho)=1}\big(2^{-r/2}\big)^2 = \frac{\#\{\rho: V(x,\rho) = 1\}}{2^r} = \Pr[M(x)\text{ accepts}].$$ So the $\tfrac23/\tfrac13$ guarantee of $M$ carries over to $C_n$. Uniformity follows as in the theorem $\mathsf{P}\subseteq\mathsf{BQP}$. ∎

Remark (Why the coins stay classical)

In this proof the coin register itself is a permanent which-path record: nothing ever applies $H$ to it again, so the $2^r$ branches never interfere, and the quantum circuit behaves exactly like the randomized machine. (For the same reason, using the "garbage" circuit $R_{D_n}$ instead of $B_{D_n}$ would give the same acceptance probability; see the garbage exercise.) Getting more than $\mathsf{BPP}$ out of a quantum computer requires letting branches interfere. That is what the algorithms on the next chapter do, and there, clean computation is essential.

Exercises

Exercise

Give a circuit of two CNOTs that maps $\ket{a}\ket{b}\ket{0}\mapsto\ket a\ket b\ket{a\oplus b}$. Why doesn't it need any uncomputation?

$\CNOT$ from qubit 1 to qubit 3, then $\CNOT$ from qubit 2 to qubit 3. No scratch qubits are used, so there is no garbage to remove. (XOR is "already reversible" given its inputs.)

Exercise

In the proof of the theorem $\mathsf{BPP}\subseteq\mathsf{BQP}$, suppose we used $R_{D_n}$ (with garbage) instead of $B_{D_n}$, and measured the qubit holding $g_s$. Show the acceptance probability is unchanged.

The final state is $2^{-r/2}\sum_\rho\ket x\ket\rho\ket{g(x,\rho)}$. Distinct $\rho$ still give distinct basis states, so the probability of reading $g_s=1$ is the fraction of $\rho$ with $V(x,\rho)=1$, as before. Garbage is harmless when no interference is needed afterwards.

Exercise

Prove that $\mathsf{BQP}$ is closed under complement.

Given $\mathcal C$ for $L$, append an $X$ gate on the answer qubit. This flips the outcome, so the new acceptance probability is $1-p_{C_n}(x)$, which is $\ge\tfrac23$ exactly when $p_{C_n}(x)\le\tfrac13$, and vice versa.

Summary

  • $\mathsf{BQP}$ is the $\mathsf{BPP}$ definition with "uniform family of quantum circuits over $\mathcal G$" in place of "probabilistic TM." Its acceptance probability is $p_C(x) = \sum_{y_m=1}|\amp{y}{C}{x0^{m-n}}|^2$.
  • Toffoli gates make any classical circuit reversible, at the cost of garbage.
  • Compute, copy, uncompute: Bennett's trick removes garbage, so that the classical computation $\ket x\ket b\mapsto\ket x\ket{b\oplus f(x)}$ can be used inside an interference pattern.
  • $\mathsf{P}\subseteq\mathsf{BPP}\subseteq\mathsf{BQP}$. Coins are Hadamards whose branches are never allowed to interfere.
