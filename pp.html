The Main Theorem: $\mathsf{BQP}\subseteq\mathsf{PP}$

In this chapter we prove the main theorem of these notes, $\mathsf{BQP}\subseteq\mathsf{PP}$, due to Adleman, DeMarrais and Huang. It sharpens the previous chapter’s $\mathsf{BQP}\subseteq\mathsf{PSPACE}$ and completes the sandwich $\mathsf{BPP}\subseteq\mathsf{BQP}\subseteq\mathsf{PP}$. We will use the definition of $\mathsf{PP}$ from the Background Review, the definition of $\mathsf{BQP}$, the procedure $\mathrm{Follow}$ and the Path-Sum Lemma. You can hover over any of these links to see it again.

The theorem

Theorem (Main Theorem; Adleman, DeMarrais and Huang 1997)

$\mathsf{BQP}\subseteq\mathsf{PP}$.

More precisely: for every polynomial-time uniform circuit family $\mathcal C = (C_0, C_1, \dots)$ over $\mathcal G$ with $h = h(n)$ Hadamard gates, there is a polynomial-time probabilistic Turing machine $M$ such that for every input $x$ of length $n$, $$\Pr[M(x)\text{ accepts}] \;=\; \frac12 + \frac{2\,p_{C_n}(x) - 1}{2^{h+1}} . \tag{$\star$}$$ In particular, $\Pr[M(x)\text{ accepts}]>\tfrac12$ if and only if $p_{C_n}(x)>\tfrac12$.

Formula $(\star)$ is the easiest way to remember the theorem. The $\mathsf{PP}$ machine leans toward accepting in the same direction as the quantum circuit, but by an amount that is exponentially smaller (divided by $2^h$). That is fine, because $\mathsf{PP}$ only cares which way the machine leans, not by how much.

Why is this stronger than the theorem $\mathsf{BQP}\subseteq\mathsf{PSPACE}$? Because $\mathsf{PP}\subseteq\mathsf{PSPACE}$ (see proposition), and $\mathsf{PP}$ is not believed to be all of $\mathsf{PSPACE}$. It also tells us something new about what quantum computers are doing: quantum computation is a special kind of counting. The previous chapter only showed that a quantum computation can be replayed in polynomial space.

Why this is plausible, and the one obstacle

Here is what we have and what we need.

What $\mathsf{PP}$ can do

A PTM's acceptance probability is an average over coin sequences. Write $\mathrm{gap} = \#\text{accepting} - \#\text{rejecting}$. A $\mathsf{PP}$ machine decides whether $\mathrm{gap}>0$. So $\mathsf{PP}$ can decide the sign of a signed count, where each coin sequence contributes $+1$ or $-1$ (the Signed Counting Lemma below makes this precise).

What the quantum computer gives us

By the Signed Counts Corollary, an amplitude is a signed count: $\amp yCz = 2^{-h/2}A_y$, where $A_y$ = #positive paths to $y$ $-$ #negative paths to $y$. But the acceptance probability is a sum of squares, $\sum_{y_m=1}A_y^2/2^h$, and a square is not a signed count of single paths.

The way around the square is algebra you already know: $\big(\sum_b a_b\big)^2 = \sum_{b,b'}a_ba_{b'}$. A square of a sum over paths is a sum over pairs of paths. So the $\mathsf{PP}$ machine should guess two paths $b,b'$ at once, and each pair contributes the product of the two signs. Two more points make this work:

  • Only pairs with the same endpoint count. When we expand $\sum_y A_y^2$, a pair $(b,b')$ contributes only if both paths end at the same $y$, so the machine must “abstain” on all other pairs. (This is the interference principle again.)
  • Accept minus reject in one sum. Instead of comparing $p$ with $\tfrac12$, we compute $2p-1 = p-(1-p)$, a single signed sum in which rejecting outcomes ($y_m=0$) get an extra minus sign.

The proof has three steps:

  1. The Signed Counting Lemma (signed counting is in $\mathsf{PP}$). A PTM can decide the sign of any efficiently computable sum of $\pm1$ and $0$ terms.
  2. The Two-Path Lemma. The quantum bias $2p_C(x)-1$ is such a sum, over pairs of paths.
  3. Combine. Together they give $(\star)$.

Step 1: signed counting is in PP

Lemma (Signed Counting Lemma; the abstain trick)

Let $q:\mathbb N\to\mathbb N$ be polynomially bounded and computable from $1^n$ in polynomial time (for example, a polynomial). Let $v(x,u)\in\{-1,0,+1\}$ be computable in polynomial time, for $x\in\{0,1\}^*$ and $u\in\{0,1\}^{q(|x|)}$. Define $$f(x) = \sum_{u\in\{0,1\}^{q(|x|)}}v(x,u).$$ Then there is a polynomial-time PTM $M$ with $\Pr[M(x)\text{ accepts}] = \dfrac12 + \dfrac{f(x)}{2^{q(|x|)+1}}$ for every $x$. In particular, $\{x: f(x)>0\}\in\mathsf{PP}$.

Proof. We define $M$ as follows:
def $M(x)$:
$q\gets q(|x|)$ (polynomial time, by assumption)
$u\gets$ $q$ random bits; $\ c\gets\mathrm{Bern}(1/2)$
if $v(x,u) = +1$: accept
if $v(x,u) = -1$: reject
if $v(x,u) = 0$: (abstain) if $c = 0$: accept else: reject
Given $u$, the probability of accepting is $1$, $0$ or $\tfrac12$ in the three cases. In every case that equals $\frac{1+v(x,u)}{2}$. Averaging over $u$: $$\Pr[M(x)\text{ accepts}] = \frac{1}{2^q}\sum_{u}\frac{1+v(x,u)}{2} = \frac12 + \frac{1}{2^{q+1}}\sum_u v(x,u) = \frac12+\frac{f(x)}{2^{q+1}}.$$ $M$ runs in polynomial time. If $f(x)>0$ it accepts with probability $>\tfrac12$, and if $f(x)\le0$ with probability $\le\tfrac12$. So $M$ witnesses $\{x:f(x)>0\}\in\mathsf{PP}$ (see definition). ∎

A probability can never be negative. But if we measure everything relative to $\tfrac12$ (accept counts as $+1$, reject as $-1$, and a fair coin flip as $0$), a PTM can add up positive and negative terms. This is how the negative numbers of quantum computation get into a classical machine. Functions of the form $f$ are called $\mathsf{GapP}$ functions (Fenner, Fortnow and Kurtz 1994).

Step 2: the two-path formula

Fix a circuit $C$ over $\mathcal G$ on $m$ qubits with $h$ Hadamard gates and an input $x\in\{0,1\}^n$, and let $z = x0^{m-n}$ as in the BQP ⊆ PSPACE chapter. Abbreviate $w(b) = w(z,b)$ and $\varphi(b) = \varphi(z,b)$, the outputs of $\mathrm{Follow}(z,b)$.

Lemma (Two-Path Lemma)

For $b,b'\in\{0,1\}^h$ define $$v(b,b') = \begin{cases}0 & \text{if } w(b)\ne w(b'),\\[3pt] (-1)^{\varphi(b)+\varphi(b')+1+w(b)_m} & \text{if } w(b) = w(b').\end{cases}$$ Then $$2\,p_C(x) - 1 \;=\; \frac{1}{2^h}\sum_{b,b'\in\{0,1\}^h}v(b,b') .$$

Reading the sign: $(-1)^{\varphi(b)}$ and $(-1)^{\varphi(b')}$ are the signs of the two paths, and $(-1)^{1+w(b)_m}$ is $+1$ if the common endpoint accepts ($w(b)_m=1$) and $-1$ if it rejects.

Proof.

Accepting minus rejecting. Since $C$ is unitary, $C\ket z$ is a unit vector (see fact), so $\sum_y\amp yCz^2 = 1$. Here we used that amplitudes are real (see corollary). Splitting by the last bit, $p_C(x) = \sum_{y_m=1}\amp yCz^2$ and $1 - p_C(x) = \sum_{y_m=0}\amp yCz^2$. Subtracting, $$2p_C(x) - 1 = \sum_{y\in\{0,1\}^m}(-1)^{1+y_m}\,\amp yCz^2 . \tag{1}$$

Square as pairs. By the Signed Counts Corollary, $\amp yCz = 2^{-h/2}\sum_b[w(b)=y](-1)^{\varphi(b)}$, where $[\cdot]$ is $1$ if the condition holds and $0$ otherwise. Squaring the sum, $$\amp yCz^2 = \frac{1}{2^h}\sum_{b,b'}[w(b)=y]\,[w(b')=y]\,(-1)^{\varphi(b)+\varphi(b')} . \tag{2}$$

Swap the sums. Substitute (2) into (1) and exchange the order of summation: $$2p_C(x)-1 = \frac1{2^h}\sum_{b,b'}(-1)^{\varphi(b)+\varphi(b')}\sum_y[w(b)=y]\,[w(b')=y]\,(-1)^{1+y_m}.$$ For a fixed pair $(b,b')$, the inner sum over $y$ has at most one nonzero term, namely $y = w(b)$. That term is present exactly when $w(b') = w(b)$, and it equals $(-1)^{1+w(b)_m}$. So the inner sum times $(-1)^{\varphi(b)+\varphi(b')}$ is exactly $v(b,b')$. ∎

Remark (Why it works)

Think of $\amp yCz^2$ as counting the ways to pick two paths that both end at $y$, with signs. Two paths that end in different places never add to the same amplitude, so they have nothing to do with each other. That is why the $\mathsf{PP}$ machine can safely abstain on those pairs. All of the interesting interference happens between paths with the same endpoint, and the signs $(-1)^{\varphi(b)+\varphi(b')}$ keep track of it exactly.

Step 3: proof of the theorem

Proof of the Main Theorem. Let $L\in\mathsf{BQP}$ via a polynomial-time uniform circuit family $\mathcal C = (C_0, C_1, \dots)$ over $\mathcal G$ (see definition). For an input $x$ of length $n$, let $m$, $T$, $h$ be the numbers of qubits, gates and Hadamards of $C_n$, and let $z = x0^{m-n}$.

Define the signed count. Let $q(n) = 2h(n)$. This is at most $2T(n)$, so it is polynomially bounded, and it can be computed from $1^n$ in polynomial time by printing $C_n$ and counting its Hadamard gates. For $u\in\{0,1\}^{2h}$, split $u = (b,b')$ into two halves and let $v(x,u) = v(b,b')$, defined as in the Two-Path Lemma for the circuit $C_n$ and start string $z$.

$v$ is computable in polynomial time. Given $(x,u)$: compute $C_n$ from $1^n$ (uniformity), run $\mathrm{Follow}(z,b)$ and $\mathrm{Follow}(z,b')$ (time $O(T+m)$ each, by the definition of $\mathrm{Follow}$), compare the endpoints, and compute the sign. Every step takes time polynomial in $n$.

The sum is the quantum bias. By the Two-Path Lemma, $$f(x) := \sum_{u\in\{0,1\}^{2h}}v(x,u) = \sum_{b,b'}v(b,b') = 2^h\big(2p_{C_n}(x)-1\big).$$

Apply the Signed Counting Lemma. It gives a polynomial-time PTM $M$ with $$\Pr[M(x)\text{ accepts}] = \frac12 + \frac{f(x)}{2^{2h+1}} = \frac12 + \frac{2^h(2p_{C_n}(x) - 1)}{2^{2h+1}} = \frac12 + \frac{2p_{C_n}(x)-1}{2^{h+1}},$$ which is $(\star)$.

Conclude. If $x\in L$, then $p_{C_n}(x)\ge\tfrac23$, so $2p_{C_n}(x)-1\ge\tfrac13>0$ and $\Pr[M(x)\text{ accepts}]>\tfrac12$. If $x\notin L$, then $p_{C_n}(x)\le\tfrac13$, so $2p_{C_n}(x)-1\le-\tfrac13<0$ and $\Pr[M(x)\text{ accepts}]<\tfrac12$. So $x\in L\iff\Pr[M(x)\text{ accepts}]>\tfrac12$, and $L\in\mathsf{PP}$. ∎

It helps to see the PTM $M$ all in one place. It is short:

def $M(x)$:
compute $C = C_{|x|}$; read off $m$ and $h$; set $z\gets x0^{m-|x|}$
$b\gets$ $h$ random bits; $\ b'\gets$ $h$ random bits; $\ c\gets\mathrm{Bern}(1/2)$
$(w,\varphi)\gets\mathrm{Follow}(z,b)$; $\ (w',\varphi')\gets\mathrm{Follow}(z,b')$
if $w\ne w'$: (the two paths can't interfere: abstain)
if $c = 0$: accept else: reject
else:
if $\varphi\oplus\varphi'\oplus 1\oplus w_m = 0$: accept else: reject

The last test says: accept if and only if (sign of $b$) $\times$ (sign of $b'$) $\times$ ($+1$ if $w$ accepts, $-1$ if not) $=+1$.

In words: guess two paths of the quantum circuit. If they end in different places, flip a coin. If they end in the same place, multiply their two signs, flip the result if the endpoint rejects, and accept if you get $+1$.

A worked example, and an explorer

Example ($M$ for the circuit $H,H$)

Take $C = H,H$ on $m=1$ qubit, run on the empty input ($z = 0$). Then $C\ket0 = \ket0$, so $p = 0$ (the last qubit never reads $1$), and $(\star)$ predicts $\Pr[M\text{ accepts}] = \tfrac12 + \tfrac{0-1}{2^3} = \tfrac38$. Let's check directly. From the $\mathrm{Follow}$ example: $w(b_1b_2) = b_2$ and $\varphi(b_1b_2) = b_1\wedge b_2$.

  • Different endpoints ($b_2\ne b_2'$): $8$ of the $16$ pairs. $v=0$, and $M$ abstains.
  • Both end at $0$ ($b_2=b_2'=0$): $4$ pairs, all with $\varphi=\varphi'=0$ and $w_m = 0$, so $v = (-1)^{0+0+1+0} = -1$.
  • Both end at $1$ ($b_2=b_2'=1$): $4$ pairs with $v = (-1)^{b_1+b_1'+1+1} = (-1)^{b_1+b_1'}$, which is $+1,-1,-1,+1$. These sum to $0$: this is the cancellation $\amp1{HH}0 = 0$, now seen in pairs.

So $f = -4 = 2^2(2\cdot0-1)$, as the Two-Path Lemma promises. Counting $M$'s $2\cdot16 = 32$ coin sequences: the abstaining pairs give $8$ acceptances, the two $v=+1$ pairs give $2\cdot 2 = 4$, and the six $v=-1$ pairs give $0$. That is $12/32 = 3/8$. ✓

The explorer below runs $M$ by brute force on all $2\cdot4^h$ coin sequences for any small circuit, and checks the count against $(\star)$. Try "Hadamards around a Toffoli," where $p$ is strictly between $0$ and $1$, or type your own circuit.

Remark (A whisper of a bias, and why this doesn't give $\mathsf{BQP}\subseteq\mathsf{BPP}$)

The quantum circuit is right with probability at least $\tfrac23$, so it is at least $\tfrac16$ away from $\tfrac12$. The $\mathsf{PP}$ machine is only $|2p-1|/2^{h+1}$ away from $\tfrac12$, which can be as small as about $\tfrac13\cdot 2^{-(h+1)}$. To notice a difference that small by running $M$ over and over, you would need about $4^h$ runs. So this is a counting argument, not a fast randomized algorithm. The cancellations that a quantum computer gets for free are paid for by the extra power of $\mathsf{PP}$, which can decide a majority even when it wins by a single vote.

Remark (Where we used real amplitudes)

We used $|a|^2 = a^2$, which holds because circuits over $\mathcal G$ have real amplitudes. For gate sets with complex entries, the universality theorem lets us switch to $\mathcal G$ first. Alternatively, write $|a|^2 = a\bar a$ and expand into pairs exactly as here, which is how Adleman, DeMarrais and Huang handle general amplitudes.

Consequences

Corollary (Consequences)
  1. $\mathsf{BPP}\subseteq\mathsf{BQP}\subseteq\mathsf{PP}\subseteq\mathsf{PSPACE}$.
  2. If $\mathsf{P} = \mathsf{PP}$, then $\mathsf{P} = \mathsf{BQP}$.
  3. If $\mathsf{BPP}\ne\mathsf{BQP}$, then $\mathsf{P}\ne\mathsf{PP}$, and therefore $\mathsf{P}\ne\mathsf{PSPACE}$.
Proof. (1) Combine the theorem $\mathsf{BPP}\subseteq\mathsf{BQP}$, the Main Theorem and the proposition $\mathsf{BPP}\subseteq\mathsf{PP}\subseteq\mathsf{PSPACE}$. (2) If $\mathsf{P}=\mathsf{PP}$ then $\mathsf{P}\subseteq\mathsf{BQP}\subseteq\mathsf{PP} = \mathsf{P}$. (3) Suppose $\mathsf{P}=\mathsf{PP}$. By (2), $\mathsf{P} = \mathsf{BQP}$, and then $\mathsf{BPP}$, which sits between them, also equals $\mathsf{BQP}$. That is the contrapositive of the first implication. For the second: if $\mathsf{P} = \mathsf{PSPACE}$ then $\mathsf{PP}$, sandwiched in between, equals $\mathsf{P}$. ∎

Part 3 is the punchline of these notes. Proving that quantum computers are more powerful than classical ones would also prove $\mathsf{P}\ne\mathsf{PSPACE}$, which is one of the biggest open problems in complexity theory. So for now, the evidence for quantum advantage has to come in other forms: oracle separations and specific problems (see the evidence in the introduction) such as factoring that we believe are hard classically.

Remark (Further results, without proof)

Fortnow and Rogers (1999) strengthened the Main Theorem: $\mathsf{BQP}$ is low for $\mathsf{PP}$, that is, $\mathsf{PP}^{\mathsf{BQP}}=\mathsf{PP}$. Aaronson (2005) showed that $\mathsf{PP}$ is exactly quantum polynomial time with postselection, which explains why $\mathsf{PP}$ is the natural ceiling. Whether $\mathsf{BQP}\subseteq\mathsf{PH}$ is open. See the survey of Watrous (2008) and the References.

Summary

  1. Amplitude = signed count of paths (see lemma).
  2. Probability = square = signed count of pairs of paths with the same endpoint (the Two-Path Lemma). Pairs with different endpoints contribute nothing, because they never interfere.
  3. $\mathsf{PP}$ decides the sign of any signed count, by accepting on $+1$, rejecting on $-1$, and flipping a coin on $0$ (the Signed Counting Lemma).
  4. So $M$ accepts with probability exactly $\tfrac12+\frac{2p-1}{2^{h+1}}$: the quantum bias, shrunk exponentially but with the same sign. Consequently, proving $\mathsf{BPP}\ne\mathsf{BQP}$ would prove $\mathsf{P}\ne\mathsf{PSPACE}$.
