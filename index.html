Quantum Computation: A Complexity Theory View

Outline

  1. Introduction

    Why quantum computation is worth studying and why it is confusing at first. This page will give a short overview of what the site covers, the background I assume (linear algebra, and basic complexity classes like P, BPP and PSPACE), and the main question we are working toward: how powerful are quantum computers compared to classical ones?

  2. Qubits and Quantum States

    A qubit as a unit vector in $\mathbb{C}^2$, written as $\alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle$ with $|\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2 = 1$. I will explain Dirac notation and measurement (you see $0$ with probability $|\alpha|^2$ and $1$ with probability $|\beta|^2$). Then multi-qubit states using tensor products, why $n$ qubits need $2^n$ amplitudes, and entanglement, using the Bell state as an example of a state that is not a tensor product of single qubits.

  3. Unitary Gates

    Why quantum operations have to be unitary matrices (they are exactly the matrices that keep the length of a state equal to $1$). I will go through the common gates: $X$, $Z$, the Hadamard gate $H$, phase gates, and the two-qubit CNOT gate, with worked examples of each one acting on a state. This page will also show how CNOT and $H$ together create entanglement.

  4. Quantum Circuits vs. Randomized Computation

    How quantum circuits are a generalization of randomized computation. A randomized computation can be described by probability vectors and stochastic matrices, while a quantum computation uses amplitude vectors and unitary matrices. The key difference is that amplitudes can be negative or complex, so they can cancel out (interference). I will use the example of applying $H$ twice, which gets back to $|0\rangle$, while flipping a fair coin twice stays random.

  5. Reversible Computation

    Unitary gates are always reversible, but gates like AND are not. This page shows how any classical circuit can still be simulated by a quantum circuit: using the Toffoli gate to make AND reversible, keeping extra "garbage" bits, and then uncomputing the garbage. This gives $\mathsf{P} \subseteq \mathsf{BQP}$, and with Hadamard gates used as coin flips, $\mathsf{BPP} \subseteq \mathsf{BQP}$.

  6. BQP and PSPACE

    The formal definition of $\mathsf{BQP}$ (problems solved by polynomial size quantum circuits with bounded error), and a proof that $\mathsf{BQP} \subseteq \mathsf{PSPACE}$. The idea is that the final amplitude can be written as a sum over exponentially many paths, and we can add up the paths one at a time while reusing space. I will finish with the known picture $\mathsf{BPP} \subseteq \mathsf{BQP} \subseteq \mathsf{PP} \subseteq \mathsf{PSPACE}$ and what is still open.

  7. Conclusion and References

    A short summary of what quantum computers can and cannot do from a complexity point of view, and why the question "is quantum computing more powerful?" is still not fully answered. This page will also list all the sources I used.
