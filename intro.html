Why Quantum Complexity?

Where quantum computation fits in 455, and in complexity theory more broadly.

Where this page sits. This is the motivation page: no theorems, just the questions the rest of the site answers. Next, Page 2 reviews the math we need, and Page 3 starts the model.

The thesis that 455 quietly relies on

Every time 455 says "$\mathsf{P}$ is the class of efficiently solvable problems," it is making a claim about the physical world, not just about Turing machines. The claim is called the Extended (or Strong) Church–Turing Thesis:

Extended Church–Turing Thesis (ECT). Any "physically reasonable" model of computation can be simulated by a (probabilistic) Turing machine with at most polynomial overhead in time.

The ordinary Church–Turing thesis is about what can be computed. The extended version is about how fast. It is why we can be casual about the model in 455: single-tape or multi-tape TMs, RAM machines, Boolean circuits, Python programs. They all simulate each other with polynomial slowdown, so $\mathsf{P}$ and $\mathsf{BPP}$ come out the same.

Notice that the thesis says "probabilistic." Randomness was the first serious candidate for a resource that might break the "deterministic" version of the thesis, and 455 handles it by defining $\mathsf{BPP}$ and asking whether $\mathsf{P} = \mathsf{BPP}$. (Most complexity theorists now believe they are equal, because of derandomization results.)

Quantum mechanics challenges the thesis

Physics, as far as we know, is quantum mechanical. In 1982 Richard Feynman pointed out that simulating a quantum system of $m$ particles on a classical computer seems to require keeping track of about $2^m$ numbers (we will see why on Page 3), and asked the obvious question: if nature is "doing" that computation for free, can we harness it? This led to a sequence of results that every complexity theorist knows:

  • 1985, Deutsch: a formal model of a quantum Turing machine.
  • 1993, Bernstein–Vazirani: the class $\mathsf{BQP}$ ("bounded-error quantum polynomial time"), the quantum analogue of $\mathsf{BPP}$. They also proved the first simulation results, and gave an oracle problem that quantum computers solve much faster than classical ones.
  • 1994, Simon: an oracle problem with an exponential quantum speed-up over randomized algorithms.
  • 1994, Shor: polynomial-time quantum algorithms for factoring integers and computing discrete logarithms.

Shor's algorithm is the reason this matters outside theory. Factoring is not known to be in $\mathsf{BPP}$; the best known classical algorithms take time roughly $2^{\tilde O(n^{1/3})}$ on $n$-bit numbers. The security of RSA rests on factoring being hard. So if large quantum computers can be built and factoring is not in $\mathsf{BPP}$, then the Extended Church–Turing Thesis is false, and "efficiently computable" means $\mathsf{BQP}$, not $\mathsf{BPP}$.

Intuition (a common misconception)

Quantum computers do not work by "trying all answers in parallel." If they did, they would solve $\mathsf{NP}$-complete problems easily, and there is good evidence that they can't (see Fact 7.8). A quantum state can indeed involve exponentially many classical strings at once, but a measurement shows you just one of them, at random. The real resource is subtler: quantum algorithms arrange for the "wrong" computation paths to cancel each other out. This site is largely about making that one sentence precise.

Where this fits in 455

In 455 you built up the map of classes $\mathsf{L} \subseteq \mathsf{NL} \subseteq \mathsf{P} \subseteq \mathsf{NP} \subseteq \mathsf{PSPACE} \subseteq \mathsf{EXP}$, and added randomness to get $\mathsf{P} \subseteq \mathsf{BPP} \subseteq \mathsf{PSPACE}$. The two questions we answer

Complexity theory pins down a new class by proving inclusions from both sides. For $\mathsf{BQP}$:

How powerful at least?

Lower bounds on power: $\mathsf{P}\subseteq\mathsf{BPP}\subseteq\mathsf{BQP}$ (Page 6). So quantum computers are no weaker than classical ones. That isn't obvious, because quantum operations must be reversible! Then evidence that they are strictly stronger: query separations (Page 7).

How powerful at most?

Upper bounds: $\mathsf{BQP}\subseteq\mathsf{PSPACE}$ (Page 8) and the sharper $\mathsf{BQP}\subseteq\mathsf{PP}$ (Page 9). So quantum computers do not violate the ordinary Church–Turing thesis, and they can be simulated by a classical machine that can count.

The upper bounds matter just as much as the algorithms. They tell us that quantum computers cannot be too powerful, and they explain why nobody has proved that quantum computers beat classical ones: by the centerpiece theorem, such a proof would also show $\mathsf{P} \neq \mathsf{PP}$, and that would be a major breakthrough in classical complexity theory (see Corollary 9.5).

What this site does not do

To keep the focus on complexity theory, we leave out three things, and say so whenever they come up:

  • Physics. We take the mathematical rules (states are unit vectors, operations are unitary, measurement follows the Born rule) as axioms.
  • Shor's algorithm. It needs the quantum Fourier transform and some number theory, more than two lectures on its own. We state it (Fact 7.8) and give references.
  • Gate-set approximation (Solovay–Kitaev). We fix one convenient finite gate set and cite the theorem that says this choice doesn't matter (Fact 4.12).
Takeaways
  • "$\mathsf{P}$ (or $\mathsf{BPP}$) = efficient" depends on the Extended Church–Turing Thesis, and quantum computing is the most serious known challenge to it.
  • $\mathsf{BQP}$ is to quantum circuits what $\mathsf{BPP}$ is to coin-flipping machines.
  • The goal is to sandwich $\mathsf{BQP}$: $\mathsf{BPP}\subseteq\mathsf{BQP}\subseteq\mathsf{PP}\subseteq\mathsf{PSPACE}$.
  • The power of quantum computing comes from cancellation (interference), not from "parallel search."
